# Diversified Personal-Investment Portfolio and Six-Month Implementation Plan

## Executive conclusion

A portfolio designed around **moderate risk, stable long-term compounding, family liquidity, disciplined rebalancing, and explicit downside control** should not use 10% as its central expected return. A defensible moderate-risk allocation is more plausibly modeled at approximately:

- **5%–7% nominal annualized return before personal taxes**, net of modest implementation costs;
- **2%–4% real return** if inflation averages 2%–3%;
- **9%–11% annual volatility**;
- roughly **15%–25% drawdown in a serious but diversified-market decline**, with a possible **25%–35% severe tail drawdown**.

An alternative with approximately **85% equities** makes a 10% nominal CAGR more attainable in a favorable decade, but its central modeled return remains closer to **6%–8%**, and a severe drawdown could reach **40%–55%**. That is not naturally compatible with “moderate risk” or a strong priority on loss control.

The sound hierarchy is therefore:

1. Protect the family liquidity reserve and near-term obligations.
2. Select a portfolio whose drawdown can actually be held through.
3. Earn diversified market returns at low cost.
4. Treat 10% nominal CAGR as an **aspirational long-horizon outcome**, not a promised or required annual result.
5. If financial planning requires 10%, first change contributions, horizon, withdrawal goals, or mortgage strategy rather than forcing the portfolio into excessive equity, concentration, leverage, or speculative assets.

All figures below are illustrative planning assumptions, not guarantees or individualized regulated advice. No current yield, valuation, tax rule, or eligible product is assumed because country, currency, accounts, taxes, and exact holdings remain unspecified.

## 1. Scope and missing information

This framework assumes:

- A long-term objective, preferably **10 years or more**.
- No long-term portfolio withdrawals during the first six months.
- Emergency and near-term family funds are separated before investing.
- Broad, liquid, regulated funds are available in the investor’s jurisdiction.
- No leverage, short selling, concentrated speculation, or required income distribution.
- Portfolio reporting will be in the investor’s eventual **base or liability currency**.
- Risk tolerance is moderate, while risk capacity cannot yet be established.

The following missing facts could materially change the allocation:

- Country of residence, citizenship, tax residence, and base currency.
- Account types and tax treatment.
- Investable amount and future contributions.
- Exact horizon and first expected withdrawal date.
- Essential household spending and number of dependants.
- Income stability, number of earners, employment concentration, and access to benefits.
- Mortgage balance, rate, fixed/floating status, remaining term, tax deductibility, and prepayment penalties.
- Other debts.
- Current cash and investments.
- Insurance coverage, deductibles, exclusions, and maximum out-of-pocket costs.
- Ethical restrictions.
- Fund domicile, product eligibility, investor-compensation arrangements, and estate-tax exposure.
- Ability and willingness to tolerate a temporary portfolio loss of 15%, 25%, 40%, or more.

The SEC’s investor guidance emphasizes that asset allocation is personal and depends primarily on time horizon and both the ability and willingness to accept losses. It also explains that higher potential return entails higher risk and that no securities portfolio is risk-free. See [Investor.gov, “Asset Allocation and Diversification”](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/asset-allocation) [1] and [Investor.gov, “Beginners’ Guide to Asset Allocation, Diversification, and Rebalancing”](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/general-resources/publications-research/info-sheets/beginners-guide-asset) [2].

## 2. Define the 10% target correctly

### Recommended measurement convention

Use the following as the primary definition:

> **A 10% nominal, annualized geometric total return—CAGR—measured in the investor’s base currency over rolling ten-year periods and since inception, net of fund expenses, platform fees, trading costs, and foreign-exchange costs, but before personal taxes.**

Also report:

- Real return after inflation.
- After-tax return where it can be calculated reliably.
- Money-weighted return reflecting contributions and withdrawals.
- Performance against a strategic blended benchmark.
- Rolling three-, five-, and ten-year results, without treating a one-year shortfall as failure.

Taxes should initially be excluded from the primary portfolio comparison because they depend on unknown account type, jurisdiction, tax rate, holding period, loss-relief rules, fund domicile, and distribution policy. They should still be tracked separately.

### Nominal versus real

A nominal 10% result is not a 10% increase in purchasing power.

At 3% inflation:

\[
\text{Real return}=\frac{1.10}{1.03}-1\approx 6.8\%
\]

At 5% inflation:

\[
\frac{1.10}{1.05}-1\approx 4.8\%
\]

Conversely, a target of **10% real** would require approximately 13.3% nominal when inflation is 3%, before considering tax and fees. That would be implausible as a planning assumption for a moderate-risk diversified portfolio.

### Arithmetic return versus CAGR

Arithmetic average return is the simple average of periodic results. CAGR reflects compounding and is usually the more relevant measure for wealth growth.

For example:

- Year 1: +20%
- Year 2: –20%

The arithmetic average is 0%, but the investment ends at:

\[
1.20\times0.80=0.96
\]

That is a loss of 4%, or approximately –2.0% CAGR.

A rough approximation is:

\[
\text{Geometric return}\approx\text{Arithmetic return}
-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2
\]

where \(\sigma\) is annual volatility. Thus, a portfolio with 10% arithmetic return and 16% volatility has an approximate geometric return of only:

\[
10\%-\frac{1}{2}(16\%)^2\approx8.7\%
\]

This “volatility drag” is one reason a 10% CAGR is harder to achieve than a 10% arithmetic expected return.

### Gross versus net

If a portfolio earns 7% gross and loses 0.4% annually to total costs, the pre-tax net return is approximately 6.6%. Over 20 years:

- 7.0% compounds one unit to about 3.87.
- 6.6% compounds it to about 3.58.

Taxes can create a much larger gap, especially where distributions, realized gains, interest, and foreign dividends are taxed annually. Investor.gov cautions that apparently small fees can materially affect long-term outcomes [3].

### How the target should be used

The target should be:

- A long-term aspiration and planning reference.
- Evaluated over at least a full market cycle.
- Compared with actual risk taken.
- Revisited if capital-market conditions, inflation, or personal circumstances change.

It should not be:

- An annual minimum.
- A reason to abandon diversification.
- A trigger for performance chasing.
- A justification for leverage, concentrated stocks, illiquid schemes, or unregulated products.
- Used to judge a portfolio after only six months or one year.

## 3. Is 10% compatible with the stated preferences?

### Moderate-risk portfolio

A genuinely moderate allocation containing 45%–60% equities, high-quality bonds, short-duration instruments, and cash is unlikely to have a defensible central expectation of 10% CAGR.

Its nominal return may exceed 10% in strong years and favorable decades. But planning on 10% would normally require one or more of the following:

- A very high equity allocation.
- Significant small-cap, value, emerging-market, or concentration risk.
- A favorable starting valuation and economic outcome.
- Leverage.
- Illiquid or complex assets.
- A willingness to sustain severe drawdowns.
- Unusually low costs and taxes.

The last four conflict particularly strongly with the preference for explicit downside control.

### Return-seeking portfolio

An 80%–90% equity portfolio makes 10% nominal CAGR **possible**, not probable or dependable. A favorable decade could produce it, but stagnation or adverse valuations could leave CAGR far below target.

It also creates a behavioral problem. An investor who changes strategy after a 35%–50% loss can permanently impair compounding. A lower-return portfolio that can be held consistently may produce a better realized result than a high-return design abandoned during a crash.

### Which objective must yield?

Under adverse conditions, all three cannot be maintained simultaneously:

1. A 10% annualized target.
2. Moderate risk and strong downside control.
3. Full liquidity and no leverage.

Given the stated priorities, **the 10% target should yield first**. If the target is essential to a financial goal, the preferred adaptations are:

- Increase contributions.
- Extend the horizon.
- Reduce or delay withdrawals.
- Reduce the target amount.
- Reassess mortgage repayment.
- Accept explicitly—and in advance—a materially larger drawdown.

## 4. Separate family liquidity from the investment portfolio

### Emergency-reserve formula

Do not use an invented fixed monetary amount. Define:

\[
R=(E\times M)+K+D-I
\]

where:

- \(E\) = essential monthly expenditure;
- \(M\) = appropriate months of coverage;
- \(K\) = known near-term costs;
- \(D\) = insurance deductibles and credible uncovered risks;
- \(I\) = reliable immediately available inflows or separate reserves.

Essential spending should include:

- Mortgage or rent.
- Utilities, food, transport, and healthcare.
- Insurance premiums.
- Childcare, education, or dependant support.
- Minimum debt payments.
- Taxes and essential property costs.
- Necessary maintenance.
- Other non-discretionary family commitments.

### Selecting the number of months

A lower range may be reasonable when there are:

- Two independent, stable incomes.
- Strong employment protections.
- Low debt.
- Broad insurance coverage.
- Flexible discretionary spending.
- No large expected withdrawals.

A higher range is appropriate when there are:

- One primary income.
- Variable, self-employed, commission-based, or cyclical income.
- Dependants.
- A floating-rate mortgage.
- High insurance deductibles.
- Employment and portfolio risks tied to the same industry.
- Expected home, healthcare, education, or family expenses.
- Limited access to social insurance or credit.

A broad starting range is **three to twelve months of essential expenditure**, but the variables above—not a generic rule—determine the final number.

### Where the reserve should be held

Suitable reserve vehicles may include:

- Insured bank deposits.
- Regulated savings accounts.
- Short-term government bills held to maturity.
- A short ladder of sovereign bills or deposits.
- A high-quality government money-market fund, subject to local regulation.

The reserve should prioritize:

1. Principal stability.
2. Same-currency access.
3. Immediate or scheduled liquidity.
4. Deposit or custody protection.
5. Low fees.
6. Yield only after the other requirements are met.

A money-market fund is normally a security rather than an insured bank deposit, and local protections differ. A bond fund is not cash: even a high-quality short-duration fund can decline when rates rise or liquidity deteriorates.

Funds required within approximately three years generally should not depend on equity-market recovery.

## 5. Mortgage prepayment decision

Mortgage prepayment may dominate investing on a risk-adjusted basis, depending on the unknown rate and tax treatment.

### Relevant comparison

Estimate the prepayment return as:

\[
\text{Effective return}=
\frac{\text{interest avoided}-\text{tax benefits lost}-\text{fees}}
{\text{principal prepaid}}
\]

Compare this with:

- The after-tax yield on same-currency government bills or insured deposits.
- The expected after-tax return of the proposed portfolio.
- The portfolio’s volatility and drawdown risk.
- The value of preserving liquidity.

### Decision branches

**High-rate, non-deductible mortgage**

Prepayment provides a relatively predictable return equal to the interest avoided and may be superior to adding bonds or taking equity risk to pursue a higher uncertain return.

**Low-rate, fixed mortgage**

Maintaining the mortgage and investing can be reasonable if:

- The rate is fixed for a long period.
- Liquidity is already sufficient.
- No near-term repayment risk exists.
- The investor can tolerate portfolio losses.
- Expected after-tax investment return adequately exceeds the mortgage rate.

**Floating or soon-to-reset mortgage**

Use a higher liquidity reserve and stress-test the payment at materially higher rates. Partial prepayment may function as downside control.

**Deductible mortgage interest**

Use the actual net after-tax cost, not the headline rate. Deductibility may be capped, phased out, or unavailable depending on jurisdiction and use of the property.

**Prepayment penalties or loss of flexible redraw facilities**

These can reduce or reverse the advantage. Prepaid capital also becomes illiquid unless the mortgage permits redraw.

The emergency reserve should generally be established before making irreversible mortgage prepayments.

### Stocks

Stocks are the main long-term growth engine but also the main drawdown source. Investor.gov notes that stocks historically have had higher risk and return than bonds or cash, while losses can be dramatic [2].

Broad equity funds are preferable for the core because they spread exposure across companies, industries, and countries. Direct stock selection should be optional and tightly limited.

### Bonds

High-quality bonds provide:

- Contractual income.
- Lower volatility than equities.
- A source of funds for rebalancing.
- Potential gains during some recessions and deflationary shocks.

They are not guaranteed to rise when stocks decline. During an inflation or rate shock, stocks and bonds may fall together. Long-duration bonds can experience large mark-to-market losses even with little credit risk.

### Cash and short-duration government instruments

These provide liquidity and reduce drawdowns but create long-run inflation and reinvestment risk. Cash should not be expected to deliver equity-like returns.

### Mutual funds and ETFs

Mutual funds and ETFs are vehicles, not separate asset classes. Both can deliver broad diversification. Investor.gov warns that narrowly focused funds may not diversify a portfolio and recommends checking top holdings for overlap [1].

**ETF advantages**

- Intraday trading.
- Often low expense ratios.
- Transparent holdings.
- Portability in many brokerage systems.

**ETF risks and costs**

- Bid-ask spread.
- Premium or discount to net asset value.
- Brokerage and foreign-exchange costs.
- Temptation to overtrade.
- Fund-domicile and tax complications.

**Mutual-fund advantages**

- Automatic fractional purchases.
- Convenient periodic contributions.
- Trading at net asset value.
- Potentially simpler rebalancing.

**Mutual-fund disadvantages**

- Possible minimums.
- Potentially higher costs.
- Less portability.
- Possible entry, exit, distribution, or platform charges.

Choose on **all-in cost, diversification, tracking, tax treatment, regulation, and operational suitability**, not the ETF or mutual-fund label.

### Real assets and alternatives

A small allocation to gold, listed infrastructure, or commodities may improve diversification in certain shocks. None is a dependable hedge in every crisis. Property funds are often equity-like, while gold and commodities can have long periods of weak or negative real returns.

Complex private assets are not necessary for this plan and may introduce valuation opacity, lockups, leverage, high fees, and minimum-investment problems.

## 7. Recommended strategic allocation

## Moderate-risk core portfolio

| Asset class | Strategic target | Operating range |
|---|---:|---:|
| Developed-market broad equities | 40% | 35%–45% |
| Emerging-market equities | 8% | 5%–12% |
| Global small-cap/value tilt | 7% | 4%–10% |
| High-quality intermediate bonds | 20% | 15%–25% |
| Inflation-linked government bonds | 5% | 3%–8% |
| Short-duration government instruments | 10% | 7%–15% |
| Portfolio cash/money market | 5% | 3%–8% |
| Gold or another carefully selected diversifier | 5% | 0%–7% |
| **Total** | **100%** | |

This portfolio is:

- 55% equities.
- 35% bonds and short sovereign instruments.
- 5% portfolio cash.
- 5% alternative diversifier.

The 5% portfolio-cash allocation is separate from the family emergency reserve. If the reserve is already conservatively invested and much larger than necessary, portfolio cash could be reduced and high-quality bonds increased.

### Simplified implementation

Where portfolio size, tax rules, or product availability favors simplicity:

- 50%–60% global broad equity index fund.
- 25%–35% base-currency-hedged high-quality bond index fund.
- 10%–15% short government bills/cash.
- Optional 0%–5% diversifier.

An appropriate low-cost balanced or target-risk fund can also implement the portfolio, provided its underlying allocation, fees, tax treatment, rebalancing process, and equity exposure are understood.

## Higher-return alternative

| Asset class | Strategic target | Operating range |
|---|---:|---:|
| Developed-market broad equities | 60% | 55%–65% |
| Emerging-market equities | 15% | 10%–18% |
| Small-cap/value equities | 10% | 7%–13% |
| High-quality bonds | 10% | 7%–15% |
| Short government instruments | 3% | 2%–5% |
| Portfolio cash | 2% | 1%–4% |
| **Total** | **100%** | |

This is an **85% equity portfolio**. It should be classified as growth-oriented or aggressive rather than moderate.

It makes a 10% nominal CAGR more plausible in a favorable scenario, but:

- It still does not justify expecting 10%.
- It could lose approximately 30%–40% in a conventional equity crash.
- A severe global crisis could produce a 40%–55% peak-to-trough loss.
- It offers substantially less help during a sequence of poor equity returns.

Do not adopt it unless the investor could continue holding and contributing after a loss of at least 40%.

## 8. Diversification rules within the allocation

### Geography

Within equities:

- Developed markets: approximately **70%–85% of equity exposure**.
- Emerging markets: approximately **10%–20%**.
- Small/frontier or specialized markets: limited to the balance.

A home-country allocation can reduce currency and behavioral discomfort, but excessive home bias creates economic, sector, regulatory, and political concentration.

A reasonable framework is:

- If the home market is broad and globally diversified: up to 20%–40% of equities.
- If it is small or concentrated: ordinarily cap it closer to 10%–25%.
- Count employer stock, local property, salary, pension, and business exposure when evaluating home-country concentration.

### Sector

Use a broad market index as the neutral reference:

- Avoid deliberate sector bets in the core.
- Keep any sector tilt within approximately **five percentage points of benchmark weight**.
- Avoid allowing one sector to exceed approximately **25% of the total equity sleeve**, unless that reflects an unusually concentrated global market and the resulting risk is consciously accepted.
- Count overlapping funds by their underlying holdings.

### Market capitalization and style

The core should remain broad-market and style-neutral. A small-cap or value tilt should normally be:

- No more than 10%–20% of the equity sleeve.
- Implemented through a diversified, rules-based fund.
- Held only if the investor can tolerate a decade or more of underperformance.
- Evaluated net of higher turnover, spreads, and tax costs.

### Currency

Assets intended to meet short-term spending and debt should normally be held in the liability currency.

For bonds:

- Hedge approximately **80%–100% of foreign bond currency exposure** to the base currency.
- Otherwise currency volatility may dominate the bond’s defensive role.

For equities:

- Unhedged global exposure can diversify long-run currency and economic risk.
- Partial hedging can be considered if foreign currency swings would make the portfolio unholdable.
- Avoid tactical currency predictions.

If 35% of a portfolio is exposed to foreign currencies, a 15% appreciation in the base currency could reduce portfolio value by roughly 5.25 percentage points before underlying asset movements:

\[
35\%\times -15\%=-5.25\%
\]

The reverse movement would help returns.

### Bond quality and duration

Within the bond allocation:

- 50%–80% sovereign or government-related.
- 20%–40% investment-grade corporate, where appropriate.
- 10%–25% inflation-linked.
- High-yield bonds: preferably 0%; otherwise no more than 10% of the bond sleeve.

Duration framework:

- Funds needed within three years: cash, bills, or duration under roughly two years.
- Core defensive bonds: average duration approximately two to seven years.
- Avoid making long-duration bonds the entire defensive allocation.
- Match known liability dates with individual sovereign bonds or deposits where practical.

## 9. Quantitative portfolio expectations

### Assumptions

The central illustrative arithmetic-return assumptions are:

| Asset | Arithmetic return | Volatility assumption |
|---|---:|---:|
| Developed equities | 8.0% | 17% |
| Emerging equities | 9.0% | 24% |
| Small/value equities | 9.0% | 22% |
| High-quality bonds | 4.5% | 6% |
| Inflation-linked bonds | 4.5% | 7% |
| Short government instruments | 3.5% | 2% |
| Cash | 3.0% | 1% |
| Gold/diversifier | 4.5% | 17% |

These are deliberately broad planning estimates. They are not current market-implied returns. Actual expected returns should be refreshed using the then-current:

- Sovereign yield curve.
- Real yields.
- Credit spreads.
- Equity earnings and valuation measures.
- Inflation expectations.
- Currency-hedging costs.
- Fund expenses and local taxes.

### Modeled results

| Metric | Moderate core | 85% equity alternative |
|---|---:|---:|
| Arithmetic nominal return | About 6.4% | About 7.7% |
| Estimated volatility | 9%–11% | 14%–17% |
| Approximate geometric return before costs | 5.7%–6.1% | 6.2%–6.7% |
| Approximate net CAGR at 0.2% annual cost | 5.5%–5.9% | 6.0%–6.5% |
| Estimated downside deviation | 6%–8% | 10%–13% |
| Normal-model probability of a negative year | Roughly 25% | Roughly 30%–32% |
| Serious drawdown | 15%–25% | 25%–40% |
| Severe tail drawdown | 25%–35% | 40%–55% |

The probability estimates use a simplified normal distribution and likely understate real tail risk, correlation spikes, and prolonged losses.

### Sharpe ratios

Using an illustrative 3% cash return:

\[
\text{Sharpe}=\frac{R_p-R_f}{\sigma_p}
\]

Moderate core:

\[
\frac{6.4\%-3.0\%}{9.5\%}\approx0.36
\]

Growth alternative:

\[
\frac{7.7\%-3.0\%}{15.5\%}\approx0.30
\]

These are planning estimates, not backtested results. Sharpe ratios can be misleading when returns are non-normal or when the risk-free rate changes.

### Sortino ratios

Using a 0% minimum acceptable return and midpoint downside-deviation estimates:

Moderate core:

\[
\frac{6.4\%-0\%}{6.5\%}\approx0.98
\]

Growth alternative:

\[
\frac{7.7\%-0\%}{11.5\%}\approx0.67
\]

Using a 3% minimum acceptable return instead:

- Moderate core: approximately 0.52.
- Growth alternative: approximately 0.41.

The result changes materially with the minimum acceptable return and observation frequency, so the dashboard must state both.

### Limitations

- Correlations change during crises.
- Government bonds may not protect against inflation-driven equity losses.
- A gold allocation may fail to hedge a particular crisis.
- Fund prices can deviate temporarily from underlying value.
- Taxes, spreads, currency conversion, and forced withdrawals can worsen outcomes.
- Historical maximum drawdown depends heavily on exact index, period, rebalancing, and currency.
- Severe outcomes can exceed the modeled figures.

## 12. Six-month implementation schedule

The purpose of staging is operational and behavioral discipline, not a belief that market timing will add value.

### Month 1: Household and policy preparation

- Calculate essential monthly expenditure.
- Size the emergency reserve.
- List known expenses over the next 24–36 months.
- Document mortgage terms, other debts, insurance gaps, and deductibles.
- Separate emergency cash from investable capital.
- Identify base currency and investment horizon.
- Write an investment-policy statement covering:
  - target allocation;
  - acceptable drawdown;
  - rebalancing rules;
  - contribution rules;
  - direct-stock limits;
  - prohibited tactics;
  - benchmark;
  - review dates.
- Confirm whether the moderate allocation remains acceptable under the –21% crash illustration.

If a 20% decline would trigger selling, reduce the equity allocation before investing.

### Month 2: Account and product due diligence

For the brokerage or platform, verify:

- Regulatory registration.
- Custody and segregation of client assets.
- Investor-compensation or deposit-protection scope.
- Insolvency treatment.
- Trading, custody, advice, inactivity, transfer, and FX fees.
- Tax reporting.
- Ability to transfer holdings to another provider.
- Beneficiary or estate arrangements.
- Fraud-prevention and account-security controls.

For each fund, read the prospectus or equivalent disclosure and check:

- Fund domicile and regulatory structure.
- Index methodology or active mandate.
- Expense ratio and actual tracking difference.
- Assets under management and closure risk.
- Bid-ask spread and normal trading volume.
- Physical versus synthetic replication.
- Derivative and counterparty exposure.
- Securities-lending policy and revenue split.
- Currency-hedged versus unhedged share class.
- Accumulating versus distributing treatment.
- Foreign withholding tax.
- Duration, yield, credit quality, and maturity distribution for bonds.
- Top holdings and overlap.
- Exit charges, minimums, and settlement time.

Investor.gov recommends checking financial professionals’ credentials and disciplinary history and warns about impersonation and relationship-investment scams [3].

### Month 3: Initial deployment

Two valid approaches exist.

**Immediate deployment**

Appropriate when:

- The reserve is complete.
- The portfolio and horizon are settled.
- A near-term decline would not cause abandonment.
- The investor accepts that markets generally offer positive expected returns.

**Fixed staged deployment**

Appropriate when:

- The funds represent a large share of family wealth.
- Behavioral regret could cause panic selling.
- Operational setup is complex.
- The investor wants to learn the mechanics gradually.

A neutral six-month schedule could invest:

- 40% of investable long-term capital in Month 3.
- 20% in Month 4.
- 20% in Month 5.
- 20% in Month 6.

Each tranche should be invested on predetermined dates regardless of headlines. Undeployed capital remains in cash or short government instruments. Do not suspend purchases because markets feel expensive or accelerate them because prices have recently risen.

### Month 4: Continue purchases and automate contributions

- Invest the second tranche.
- Direct regular contributions to the most underweight asset class.
- Automate transfers after income is received.
- Reinvest distributions where tax-efficient.
- Confirm allocation using underlying exposures, not fund names.
- Check that no sector, country, or stock limit has been breached.

Monthly contributions should normally be invested when available rather than accumulated for tactical entry, unless dealing costs make very small trades inefficient.

### Month 5: Complete operational review

- Invest the third tranche.
- Review actual fees, spreads, FX costs, and tax documentation.
- Confirm beneficiaries and account-security settings.
- Check whether portfolio volatility has affected willingness to continue.
- Correct instrument-selection mistakes, but do not change strategy because of short-term performance.
- Prepare the dashboard and benchmark.

### Month 6: Final tranche and first rebalance

- Invest the final scheduled tranche.
- Use new money to bring assets toward target weights.
- Rebalance only if a threshold has been breached.
- Record:
  - starting values;
  - cost basis;
  - expected distributions;
  - fees;
  - benchmark weights;
  - target ranges.
- Set the next formal review date.
- Do not assess whether the strategy “worked” based on six-month return.

## 13. Rebalancing policy

Investor.gov describes both calendar rebalancing—such as every six or twelve months—and preset threshold rebalancing, noting that relatively infrequent rebalancing tends to be preferable [1].

Use a combined policy:

### Calendar rule

- Monitor quarterly.
- Conduct a formal review every six months.
- Rebalance no more than once or twice per year unless a threshold or personal-risk trigger is reached.

### Threshold rule

Rebalance when either the absolute or relative threshold is exceeded:

- Major allocation above 10% target weight: **five percentage points** or **20% relative drift**.
- Allocation between 5% and 10%: approximately **two percentage points**.
- Allocation below 5%: approximately **one percentage point**.

Examples:

- A 55% equity target triggers around 50% or 60%, subject to the exact sub-asset bands.
- A 10% emerging-market target triggers near 8% or 12%.
- A 5% diversifier target triggers near 4% or 6%.

### Order of rebalancing actions

1. Redirect new contributions.
2. Use interest, dividends, and distributions.
3. Trade inside tax-advantaged accounts.
4. Sell positions with losses where local tax law permits useful loss relief.
5. Sell appreciated taxable assets only after comparing tax cost with concentration risk.
6. Use partial rebalancing if full rebalancing would create disproportionate taxes or fees.

Rebalancing controls risk; it does not guarantee higher returns.

## 14. Position and concentration limits

Suggested policy limits:

- Any individual stock: **maximum 2% of total portfolio** at purchase.
- All direct stocks combined: **maximum 10%**.
- Employer stock: preferably **no more than 5%**, counting stock options and employment exposure.
- Any single corporate bond issuer: **1%–2%**.
- Total exposure to one non-sovereign issuer across securities: **maximum 3%**.
- High-yield and unrated debt: **0%–5% of total portfolio**.
- Any thematic or sector fund: **maximum 5%**.
- All thematic positions combined: **maximum 10%**.
- Gold: normally **0%–7%**.
- Broad commodities or managed futures: normally **0%–5% each**.
- Cryptoassets, if legally eligible and insisted upon: treat as speculative and cap around **0%–2%**; they are not necessary to meet the plan.
- Illiquid investments: zero until liquidity, eligibility, valuation, fees, and exit risks are fully understood.

A broad index fund may exceed these fund-level limits because its underlying assets are diversified. Even then, consider operational concentration in a single fund family, custodian, index provider, or securities-lending counterparty.

## 15. Downside controls

### Controls supported by sound portfolio logic

**Diversification**

Spread risk across asset classes and within each asset class. Investor.gov explicitly distinguishes between these two levels of diversification [1][2].

**High-quality defensive assets**

Use government and investment-grade bonds rather than relying on high-yield bonds, which tend to behave more like equities during stress.

**Liquidity reserve**

This prevents forced sales when markets are down.

**Position sizing**

Cap single-name, employer, sector, country, and thematic exposures.

**Moderate duration**

Avoid relying entirely on long bonds for defense.

**Rebalancing**

Sell portions of assets that have risen beyond their risk budget and buy assets below target.

**No leverage**

Leverage increases forced-sale and permanent-loss risk and is inconsistent with the stated priorities.

### Optional systematic rules

A trend or volatility rule can be considered only as a small, precommitted overlay.

Example:

- Review monthly.
- If the global equity index remains below a long-term moving average, shift no more than 10%–20% of the equity sleeve to short government bills.
- Restore exposure according to the same objective rule.
- Do not override the rule based on forecasts.

Limitations:

- False signals and repeated “whipsaw” losses.
- Taxable turnover.
- Tracking error.
- Selling after an initial decline.
- Missing sharp recoveries.
- Dependence on parameter choice.

Volatility targeting can reduce exposure when measured volatility rises, but it frequently de-risks only after prices have already fallen. It should not use leverage to increase exposure during quiet periods in a moderate portfolio.

### Stop-loss orders

Stop losses are usually poor core-portfolio insurance because:

- They do not guarantee the execution price.
- Prices can gap through the stop.
- Temporary volatility can trigger sales.
- Investors may miss rapid recoveries.
- Re-entry decisions become discretionary market timing.
- They generate tax and transaction costs.

Use position limits and asset allocation rather than mechanical stops for diversified long-term funds.

### Options hedges

Protective puts can cap losses over a defined period, but:

- Premiums create a recurring return drag.
- Protection expires.
- Long-dated options can be expensive.
- The hedge may not match the portfolio.
- Tax and operational complexity can be substantial.
- Investors may overestimate protection.

Options are appropriate only if a specific, time-limited liability requires defined protection and the full premium can be treated as an insurance expense.

### Inverse and leveraged products

These are unsuitable for the strategic core because daily reset, volatility compounding, financing costs, derivative exposure, and path dependence can cause long-term returns to differ materially from the stated daily multiple.

### Market timing

Do not change allocation based on headlines, recent returns, or unsupported forecasts. Tactical changes should require a written rule and a demonstrable change in goals, horizon, risk capacity, or long-term assumptions.

## 16. Tax- and cost-aware principles

- Prefer low-cost broad-market vehicles.
- Compare total expense ratio with actual tracking difference.
- Include platform, custody, spread, commission, FX, advice, and tax costs.
- Avoid unnecessary fund turnover.
- Use tax-advantaged accounts where suitable and legally available.
- Place tax-inefficient interest-producing assets in sheltered accounts where local law makes that advantageous.
- Keep equity index funds in taxable accounts where capital-gain deferral and favorable dividend treatment apply—but only after checking local rules.
- Harvest losses only where permitted and economically useful.
- Observe wash-sale, bed-and-breakfast, superficial-loss, or equivalent anti-avoidance rules.
- Do not allow tax avoidance to preserve a dangerous concentration.
- Avoid frequent tactical rebalancing.
- Prefer contributions and distributions for rebalancing.
- Consider accumulating versus distributing share classes only after determining local tax treatment; an accumulating fund may still generate taxable deemed income.
- Examine fund domicile, foreign withholding, estate taxes, reporting status, and treaty eligibility before selecting cross-border funds.

Investor.gov identifies pre-tax deductions, tax-deferred growth, and tax-free withdrawals as possible features of tax-advantaged accounts, but its examples are U.S.-specific and should not be generalized to other jurisdictions [3].

## 17. Monitoring dashboard

Review monthly for data integrity and quarterly for risk. Make strategic decisions only at scheduled reviews or objective trigger events.

| Metric | Definition | Suggested control |
|---|---|---|
| Total return | Price change plus distributions, net of costs | Report monthly, quarterly, yearly |
| CAGR | Geometric annualized return | Since inception and rolling 3/5/10 years |
| Real CAGR | Nominal CAGR adjusted for inflation | Compare with purchasing-power goals |
| Money-weighted return | Return reflecting contribution timing | Use for personal experience |
| Time-weighted return | Removes contribution timing effect | Use against benchmark |
| Blended benchmark | Same asset weights using appropriate broad indexes | Rebalance benchmark using same policy |
| Volatility | Standard deviation of periodic returns | Compare with 9%–11% core range |
| Maximum drawdown | Largest peak-to-trough decline | Review against accepted loss budget |
| Downside deviation | Volatility of returns below stated minimum | State whether minimum is 0%, inflation, or cash |
| Sharpe ratio | Excess return divided by volatility | Use over multi-year periods only |
| Sortino ratio | Excess return divided by downside deviation | State minimum acceptable return |
| Allocation drift | Difference from strategic target | Apply threshold rules |
| Concentration | Largest stock, issuer, sector, country, fund family | Enforce policy limits |
| Fees | Weighted fund plus platform and trading costs | Investigate unexplained increases |
| Income | Interest and dividends, gross and net of tax | Do not confuse yield with total return |
| Liquidity coverage | Reserve divided by essential monthly expenses | Maintain selected month target |
| Mortgage exposure | Rate, reset date, payment stress | Review when terms change |
| Target progress | Actual capital versus goal path | Adapt contributions before taking more risk |

### Appropriate benchmark

For the moderate core, construct a benchmark matching the strategic weights:

- 40% developed global equity index.
- 8% emerging-market index.
- 7% small/value index.
- 20% high-quality base-currency or currency-hedged bond index.
- 5% inflation-linked bond index.
- 10% short government index.
- 5% cash rate.
- 5% appropriate gold or diversifier index.

Do not compare a moderate portfolio solely with an all-equity index. That would encourage taking unplanned risk.

## 18. Objective review triggers

### Personal circumstances

Review the policy immediately if:

- Employment or income stability changes.
- A dependant is added.
- Health or insurance coverage changes.
- A large withdrawal becomes likely.
- Divorce, inheritance, relocation, or retirement occurs.
- The time horizon shortens.
- The investor discovers that actual drawdown tolerance is lower than expected.

### Mortgage and debt

Review if:

- A fixed rate is due to reset.
- The mortgage becomes floating.
- Refinancing is available.
- Prepayment penalties expire.
- Tax deductibility changes.
- The after-tax mortgage rate materially exceeds defensive-asset yields.

### Markets and economics

Review long-run assumptions—but do not automatically trade—if:

- Real government yields move by approximately 1.5–2 percentage points.
- Inflation expectations become persistently inconsistent with the plan.
- Equity valuations move to historically extreme ranges.
- Credit spreads indicate substantially greater default risk.
- Currency exposure becomes misaligned with future liabilities.
- An asset class or fund changes mandate, index, domicile, fee, or tax status.

Valuation should influence expected-return assumptions and contribution planning, not produce all-in/all-out decisions.

### Portfolio-level triggers

- Allocation breaches a rebalancing threshold.
- A direct stock exceeds its limit.
- A fund’s underlying holdings create unexpected overlap.
- Weighted annual costs increase materially.
- Tracking difference remains unexplained.
- Liquidity coverage falls below the selected requirement.
- A drawdown exceeds the documented tolerance.
- Performance trails the correct benchmark substantially over a full three- to five-year period after adjusting for risk, tax, and fees.

Ordinary volatility, news, elections, predictions, and recent winners or losers are not sufficient triggers.

## 19. Jurisdiction and portfolio-size substitutes

### Taxable account

Prioritize:

- Low turnover.
- Tax-efficient broad funds.
- Contribution-based rebalancing.
- Tax-lot selection.
- Local loss-relief rules.
- Fund domicile and withholding-tax analysis.

### Retirement or tax-sheltered account

Potentially locate:

- Tax-inefficient bonds.
- Higher-distribution funds.
- Rebalancing trades.
- Strategies with otherwise taxable turnover.

Withdrawal restrictions, mandatory distributions, estate rules, and penalties must be checked locally.

### U.S.-eligible investor

Potential vehicles can include locally registered mutual funds or ETFs, Treasury bills, regulated money-market funds, and applicable tax-advantaged accounts. U.S. persons should obtain specialist advice before buying non-U.S. pooled funds because adverse tax-reporting regimes may apply.

### European or UK-style framework

Use eligible local or UCITS-regulated funds where appropriate, assess local reporting status, tax wrappers, investor documents, currency classes, and accumulating/distributing treatment. Do not assume that a U.S.-domiciled fund is accessible or tax-efficient.

### Other jurisdictions

Prefer locally regulated funds or direct sovereign instruments where global ETFs are unavailable. Verify:

- Capital controls.
- Foreign-asset reporting.
- Dividend and capital-gain taxes.
- Currency conversion.
- Treaty benefits.
- Estate rules.
- Custody and investor-compensation protections.

### Small portfolio

Use one to three broad funds:

1. Global equity.
2. High-quality bond or balanced fund.
3. Cash/short government instrument.

Avoid creating many tiny positions whose fees and administration overwhelm diversification benefits.

### Larger portfolio

Separate sleeves can improve:

- Tax-lot control.
- Duration management.
- Currency hedging.
- Factor exposure.
- Withdrawal sequencing.

Greater size does not justify greater complexity unless the expected benefit exceeds additional fees, taxes, governance burden, and behavioral risk.

## 20. Final policy recommendation

### Preferred plan

Adopt the **55% equity moderate core** after establishing the emergency reserve and resolving the mortgage comparison. Plan using approximately **5%–6% nominal net CAGR before personal taxes**, not 10%.

Measure the 10% aspiration as:

- Nominal.
- Geometric/CAGR.
- In base currency.
- Net of all investment costs.
- Before personal taxes for the primary comparison.
- Over rolling ten-year periods and since inception.

Track real and after-tax outcomes separately.

### When to use the growth alternative

Consider the 85% equity version only if all of the following are true:

- Horizon is at least 10–15 years.
- Emergency and near-term reserves are complete.
- Mortgage and other debt risks are manageable.
- No large withdrawal is likely.
- Income and insurance provide adequate risk capacity.
- A 40%–50% temporary loss would not cause selling.
- The investor explicitly accepts that downside control has become secondary to return seeking.

Even then, 10% remains a favorable outcome rather than a reliable expectation.

### Governing principle

The portfolio should not be optimized to produce the highest spreadsheet return. It should be optimized for the highest probability of being **held, funded, rebalanced, and used successfully** under adverse real-world conditions.

Diversification can reduce risk and smooth outcomes, but it cannot eliminate loss. As Investor.gov explains, return and risk are inseparable, and allocation must reflect both horizon and capacity to tolerate losses [1][2]. No allocation, rebalancing rule, hedge, or implementation schedule can guarantee a 10% return or prevent every drawdown.
