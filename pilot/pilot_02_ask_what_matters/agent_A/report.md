For a beginner doing hiking + overnight backpacking/camping + eventual mountaineering, the most cost-effective approach is a modular system: buy one dependable 3-season hiking/backpacking kit, then add or rent technical alpine equipment only when a route requires it. The National Park Service and current REI guidance both treat the Ten Essentials as the minimum baseline even for day hikes, with additional gear determined by weather, terrain and remoteness.

For packs, capacity and fit matter more than the logo. REI currently recommends roughly 50–80 L for typical 3–5-night backpacking trips, and pack sizing is based primarily on torso length rather than overall height. 
REI
+1

For footwear, your toes need some expansion room while the heel remains secure. Current fitting guidance recommends a snug-but-not-tight fit and roughly a thumb's width ahead of the longest toe. 
REI

Clothing: use a system, not one heavy jacket

The most adaptable setup is:

wicking base layer → fleece/light insulation → puffy insulation → waterproof/windproof shell.

That lets you regulate temperature during climbs, stops, rain and sudden temperature drops. It's particularly important in mountains, where the clothing you need at a windy summit may be very different from what you need while climbing uphill. 
REI

For your rain jacket, I'd spend a little extra on durability. Modern 3-layer shells generally provide the best combination of waterproof performance, breathability and abrasion resistance; 2.5-layer jackets are cheaper and lighter but typically sacrifice durability. 
REI
+1

2. Sleep system: one of the most important places to spend money

Don't evaluate a sleeping bag alone. Your warmth comes from the bag + sleeping pad + clothing.

For a first mountain-oriented setup, I'd choose:

20°F / –7°C sleeping bag + insulated pad around R 4–5.

REI's current guidance places pads below R2 in warm-weather territory, roughly R2–3.9 for cool weather, R4–5.4 for colder conditions and R5.5+ for very cold conditions. A higher-R pad also gives your sleeping bag a better chance of performing near its advertised rating. 
REI

Prefer a sleeping bag carrying an ISO or EN rating, so you can compare it meaningfully across manufacturers. If you're normally cold at night, pay more attention to the bag's comfort rating; warm sleepers can use the limit rating more heavily. 
REI

3. Water: don't depend on a filter alone everywhere

A Sawyer Squeeze, Katadyn BeFree or Platypus QuickDraw is an excellent backpacking filter, but it's important to understand what portable filters can and can't do.

The CDC recommends boiling as the most reliable treatment. Where boiling isn't practical, its next-preferred approach is filtering followed by disinfection when contamination is uncertain. Many portable microfilters remove parasites and bacteria but do not reliably remove viruses. 
CDC

So I'd carry:

primary: Sawyer/Katadyn/Platypus filter
backup: chlorine-dioxide tablets
capacity: enough bottles/reservoirs to bridge long dry stretches.

4. Cooking equipment

For one or two people backpacking, a simple remote-free canister stove setup is hard to beat:

Soto WindMaster or MSR PocketRocket + 900–1,200 mL pot + spoon + lighter + backup lighter.

The WindMaster is particularly attractive where summer mountain winds are common; the PocketRocket is less expensive and very simple.

Never use a backpacking stove inside your tent or vestibule unless the equipment manufacturer has explicitly engineered a system for such use—which ordinary backpacking setups generally have not. Standard stove instructions warn that fuel-burning appliances can generate lethal carbon monoxide in enclosed spaces.

5. Navigation and unexpected situations

A phone is an excellent navigation tool but shouldn't be the entire system.

I would carry:

phone with downloaded offline maps + paper/topographic map + Suunto/Silva baseplate compass + power bank.

Then add a satellite communicator such as Garmin inReach or ZOLEO whenever you're traveling far enough from roads or populated trails that reliable cellular service can't be assumed. The NPS explicitly recommends considering a satellite/locator device because cell phones can lack reception or lose power. 
National Park Service

And learn the compass before you need it. NPS guidance specifically emphasizes knowing how to use your topo map, compass or GPS rather than merely carrying them. 
National Park Service

6. First-aid and emergency kit

For a solo or two-person weekend, a premade Adventure Medical Kits "Hiker" or "Backpacker" kit is a good starting point. Customize it so it contains:

Blisters: leukotape/moleskin, hydrocolloid dressing
Wounds: gauze, adhesive bandages, wound-cleaning material
Bleeding: pressure dressing/gauze
Sprains: elastic wrap
Tools: tweezers, small scissors, nitrile gloves
Medication: your personal prescriptions plus any OTC medicines you normally use safely
Emergency: whistle and emergency bivy/blanket.

A first-aid kit is only as useful as your ability to use it. A basic first-aid course is useful; before serious mountaineering, a Wilderness First Aid course is an especially worthwhile investment.

Especially important: don't buy questionable used climbing PPE

Buying a used tent, backpack, trekking poles or cooking equipment can be a great way to save money.

I would not do the same with unknown-history ropes, harnesses or helmets. The UIAA's February 2026 buying guidance explicitly does not endorse second-hand climbing equipment whose integrity cannot be verified, particularly ropes, harnesses and helmets. It also recommends certified UIAA/CE equipment, correct sizing and checking system compatibility. 
UIAA

9. Where to spend versus where to save

Spend more on: footwear fit, backpack fit/suspension, sleeping pad, sleeping bag, waterproof shell, certified climbing hardware and mountaineering boots. A bad pair of shoes or a poorly fitting pack can ruin every day of a trip, while inadequate sleep/rain protection can become a safety issue.

Save on: cooking pot, spoon, fleece, hiking shirts, stuff sacks, simple gloves, basic trekking poles and some camp accessories. You don't need $100 titanium cookware to safely boil water.

Rent initially: technical mountaineering boots, crampons, ice axe, helmet, harness and group rope/rescue equipment. After taking a course and learning what type of mountaineering you actually enjoy, you'll know whether your future is mostly snow climbs, glacier travel, alpine rock or technical ice—and those require substantially different equipment.

The configuration I would choose for your goals

For a beginner wanting one durable foundation rather than chasing ultralight numbers, my target would be a 55–65 L Osprey/Gregory/Deuter pack; Merrell/Salomon hiking footwear fitted in person; 3-layer Patagonia/Outdoor Research/REI shell; REI/Big Agnes/Nemo 2-person tent; Kelty/Nemo 20°F bag; R≈4–5 insulated pad; Soto/MSR stove; Sawyer/Platypus filter; Petzl/Black Diamond headlamp; Suunto compass; Adventure Medical Kits first aid; aluminum trekking poles; emergency bivy; and a Garmin/ZOLEO communicator for remote trips.

That system is durable enough to learn on without being unnecessarily expensive, and nearly everything remains useful after you progress into mountaineering. The technical snow/glacier hardware should then be added route by route after instruction, rather than trying to build a universal alpine rack on day one.\n
