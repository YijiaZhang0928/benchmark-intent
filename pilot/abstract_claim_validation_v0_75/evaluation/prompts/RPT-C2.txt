## 3. Shelter recommendation

### For self-supported summer trekking

Start with a **robust double-wall, full-rainfly three-season tent** with:

- Reliable guy-out points
- Good pole structure
- Vestibule large enough for wet equipment
- Adequate ventilation
- Bathtub floor
- Repairable poles
- Enough space to avoid pressing the sleeping bag against wet walls
- A demonstrated pitch that you can complete quickly in rain

A freestanding or semi-freestanding design is more forgiving than a minimalist trekking-pole shelter for a new user. Do not select a tent solely because it is very light.

For wet-weather camping, avoid depressions and water channels, orient the entrance away from prevailing wind, tension and guy out the rainfly properly, and use ventilation to reduce condensation. Practicing the complete pitch at home is particularly important. [citation:How to Go Backpacking in the Rain](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/backpacking-in-the-rain.html)

### When to upgrade or rent

Rent or obtain operator-specified equipment if the confirmed route includes:

- Repeated exposed camps above treeline
- Forecast severe wind
- Meaningful snowfall
- Winter-like conditions
- Camps on snow
- Limited evacuation options

Altitude by itself does not determine whether a four-season tent is needed; exposure, wind, snow loading, campsite protection, and forecast uncertainty matter more.

## 4. Sleeping bag decision

Select a sleeping bag using its **ISO Comfort rating**, not a vague product name or only the lower-limit rating. Then build in a conservative margin below the expected nighttime minimum.

A practical provisional rule is to choose a Comfort rating approximately **5°C colder than the credible forecast low**, with additional margin for cold sleepers, exposed camps, fatigue, dampness, or uncertain forecasts.

| Credible camp minimum | Provisional bag Comfort target |
|---:|---:|
| Above +10°C | Approximately +5°C |
| +5 to +10°C | Approximately 0 to +5°C |
| 0 to +5°C | Approximately –5 to 0°C |
| –5 to 0°C | Approximately –10 to –5°C |
| Below –5°C | Route-specific cold-weather system required |

These are conservative planning targets, not a guarantee of comfort.

## 5. Sleeping pad

The sleeping bag cannot compensate fully for inadequate insulation underneath you. Select the pad as part of the complete sleep system.

Pending route-specific temperatures, use these provisional targets:

| Expected ground/night conditions | Planning target |
|---|---:|
| Mild, consistently above +5°C | ASTM R-value around 3 or higher |
| Near +5°C to freezing | Around R 4 or higher |
| Around freezing to –5°C | Around R 5 or higher |
| Colder or snow camping | Route-specific system, potentially two pads |

These are conservative selection heuristics and should be reconciled with the pad manufacturer’s current ASTM rating and the actual route.

An inflatable insulated pad provides the best balance of comfort and packed volume, but a thin closed-cell foam pad can serve as protection, camp seating, and partial backup. For remote self-supported travel, a repair kit is essential.

- NeoAir pads can be stored tightly rolled in their stuff sack with the valve open.

- Suspected punctures can be investigated and repaired using the manufacturer’s home-repair procedure.

## 6. Clothing system

Shanghai and Sichuan–Tibet impose opposing requirements: high heat and humidity during shakedowns versus potentially cold, wet, windy conditions at elevation. Solve this with removable layers, not one heavy garment.

### Core system

1. **Next-to-skin layer:** lightweight synthetic or wool
2. **Hiking shirt:** quick-drying, sun-protective
3. **Light fleece:** active insulation
4. **Hooded insulated jacket:** static warmth at camp and during stops
5. **Waterproof hard shell:** wind and rain protection
6. **Rain pants:** preferably full length
7. **Hiking trousers:** quick-drying, not cotton
8. **Dry sleep base layers:** carried in a sealed dry bag
9. **Warm hat and gloves**
10. **Sun hat and UV-rated sunglasses**

REI specifically warns against cotton in wet conditions because it dries slowly and can contribute to chilling. It recommends wool, nylon, or polyester, a true waterproof hard shell rather than a soft shell alone, and a separately protected dry clothing set for camp and sleep. [citation:How to Go Backpacking in the Rain](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/backpacking-in-the-rain.html)

Do not substitute an insulated jacket for a rain shell. Down insulation also needs waterproof internal storage even if the backpack has a rain cover.

## 7. Footwear

There is no single universally best answer for both Shanghai humidity and cold, wet high-altitude terrain.

### Shanghai shakedowns

A breathable, non-waterproof hiking shoe or trail shoe generally manages heat and dries faster after saturation. Prioritize:

- Deep, well-spaced lugs
- Secure heel
- Wide enough toe box for downhill swelling
- No pressure points under load
- Quick-drying upper
- Compatibility with your chosen socks

### Sichuan–Tibet

Use route conditions to choose:

- **Maintained trails, warmer weather, modest load:** hiking shoes may be sufficient.
- **Cold mud, persistent rain, rough trail, heavier load:** waterproof mid-height boots may be more comfortable.
- **Known snow or frozen slopes:** obtain route-appropriate boots and traction through the operator; ordinary trekking footwear may not be enough.

Waterproof footwear can initially keep feet warmer and drier in cold conditions, while non-waterproof mesh footwear dries faster in milder conditions. In either case, traction matters on mud and wet rocks. [citation:How to Go Backpacking in the Rain](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/backpacking-in-the-rain.html)

Never debut footwear on the destination trek. Test it on consecutive long days, including downhill travel and wet conditions.

## 10. High-altitude safety

Equipment cannot compensate for an ascent schedule that is too aggressive.

The CDC identifies hypoxia as the central high-altitude concern and notes that susceptibility to altitude illness is not predicted by physical fitness. Any unacclimatized traveler sleeping at approximately 2,450 m or higher can be at risk. [citation:High-Altitude Travel and Altitude Illness](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/environmental-hazards-risks/high-altitude-travel-and-altitude-illness.html)

### Itinerary rules to build into the trip

The CDC summarizes Wilderness Medical Society guidance as follows:

- If possible, spend at least **2–3 nights around 2,450–2,750 m** before proceeding higher.
- Avoid going directly from low altitude to a sleeping altitude of approximately **2,750 m or higher in one day**.
- Once above 3,000 m, limit increases in sleeping altitude to roughly **500 m per night**.
- Add an acclimatization night for every additional **1,000 m** of sleeping-altitude gain.
- Use only mild exercise during the first 48 hours at altitude.
- Avoid alcohol during the first 48 hours.
[citation:High-Altitude Travel and Altitude Illness](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/environmental-hazards-risks/high-altitude-travel-and-altitude-illness.html)

These are ceilings rather than performance goals; some people need a slower ascent.

### Recognize acute mountain sickness

The CDC describes headache as the cardinal symptom, often accompanied by one or more of:

- Loss of appetite
- Dizziness
- Fatigue
- Nausea
- Vomiting

Symptoms commonly begin 2–12 hours after arrival or ascent, often during or after the first night. Do not ascend higher while symptoms are unresolved. The CDC reports that descent of at least approximately 300 m can produce rapid improvement in AMS. [citation:High-Altitude Travel and Altitude Illness](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/environmental-hazards-risks/high-altitude-travel-and-altitude-illness.html)

Discuss the itinerary and any altitude medication with a travel-medicine clinician. This is particularly important if transportation delivers you rapidly to a high sleeping altitude. Medication is not a substitute for acclimatization or descent.

A pulse oximeter can be carried as an optional trend-monitoring tool, but symptoms and function must remain central; a device reading should not be used to justify continued ascent when a person is deteriorating.

## 12. Shanghai shakedown program

Shanghai is useful for validating fit, rain management, heat management, foot care, and equipment organization. It cannot validate altitude acclimatization or sub-freezing sleep performance.

### Stage 1: day hike

Carry approximately 60% of the intended trip load and test:

- Footwear and socks
- Daypack adjustment
- Water access
- Rain shell ventilation
- Trekking-pole length
- Chafing points
- Food tolerance
- Phone and battery endurance

### Stage 2: long wet-weather day

Increase to approximately 75–80% of projected load. Deliberately test:

- Shell and rain-pants performance
- Pack liner
- Waterproof storage for electronics
- Wet-foot management
- Blister prevention
- Navigation in poor visibility
- Eating and drinking while it rains

Rain creates additional hazards including slippery ground, swollen waterways, hypothermia, and neglected hydration. Trekking poles can materially improve stability. [citation:How to Go Backpacking in the Rain](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/backpacking-in-the-rain.html)

### Stage 3: overnight camp

Test:

- Tent pitch in rain
- Condensation management
- Sleeping bag and pad
- Stove
- Water treatment
- Nighttime organization

### Stage 4: consecutive days

Complete two or more consecutive hiking days at the projected load. Look for cumulative:

- Foot swelling
- Hip or shoulder bruising
- Numbness
- Knee pain on descent
- Achilles or plantar discomfort
- Sleep deprivation
- Calorie deficit
- Skin maceration and blistering

### Stage 5: colder test

Before trusting the sleep system at altitude, test it in conditions closer to the expected nighttime temperature. If local weather cannot provide a valid test, retain greater temperature margin or rent a warmer bag.

For your present level of uncertainty, the best value strategy is **buy personal essentials, rent expensive route-dependent equipment, and delay the final backpack and sleep-system decision**.

- 25–35 L daypack for supported trekking
- 50–65 L pack only if self-supported
- Double-wall three-season tent, upgraded only for confirmed severe exposure
