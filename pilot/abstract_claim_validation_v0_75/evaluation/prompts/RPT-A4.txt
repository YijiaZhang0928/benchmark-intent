# 2. Clothing and weather protection

Use a modular layering system rather than one heavy jacket.

## Base layers
- [ ] 1–2 synthetic or merino short-sleeve shirts
- [ ] Lightweight long-sleeve sun shirt

- [ ] Lightweight long-underwear top and bottom for cold evenings and sleeping
- [ ] 2–3 pairs of wool/synthetic hiking socks

Avoid cotton for cold, wet, or remote trips because it dries slowly. Quick-drying polyester or merino is more versatile.

## Midlayer and insulation
- [ ] Lightweight grid fleece or fleece jacket
- [ ] Hooded synthetic or down insulated jacket
- [ ] Warm hat
- [ ] Light gloves
- [ ] Optional waterproof over-mitts for exposed mountain conditions

**Down versus synthetic:**
- Down is lighter and more compressible but must be protected from moisture.
- Synthetic insulation is bulkier but is generally more forgiving in persistently wet conditions.

- Adjustable hood that moves with your head
- Pit zips or other ventilation
- Durable face fabric
- Sealed seams
- Cuffs and hem that can be closed in wind

A “water-resistant” windbreaker is not a substitute for a waterproof shell in sustained rain.

# 3. Footwear and foot care

## General hiking

Choose based on terrain and load:

### Trail shoes
Best for maintained trails, warm conditions, light loads, and hikers who prefer low weight.

### Hiking shoes or mid-height boots
Better for rocky trails, mud, off-trail travel, heavier loads, and beginners who want more protection.

### Stiffer mountaineering boots
Required when using certain crampons or spending substantial time on snow and ice. These are usually uncomfortable and unnecessarily heavy for ordinary summer hiking.

- [ ] Hiking shoes or boots with a secure heel
- [ ] Approximately a thumb’s width of toe room
- [ ] Outsole with substantial lugs
- [ ] Wool/synthetic socks
- [ ] Optional thin liner socks
- [ ] Lightweight gaiters for debris and mud
- [ ] Waterproof gaiters for snow
- [ ] Blister supplies
- [ ] Camp sandals or light camp shoes, optional

Try footwear late in the day when your feet are slightly swollen, wearing the socks you plan to use. Walk downhill on an incline: your toes should not hit the front, and your heel should not lift excessively. Waterproof footwear helps in snow, wet grass, and shallow mud but can feel hot and dries slowly after becoming saturated.

# 5. Sleeping system

## Sleeping bag

Select a bag by the **coldest realistic nighttime temperature**, including elevation—not by daytime weather.

- [ ] Mummy-shaped three-season sleeping bag
- [ ] Draft collar and hood
- [ ] ISO/EN-tested temperature rating where available
- [ ] Waterproof stuff sack or pack liner
- [ ] Optional storage sack for uncompressed storage at home

For mixed summer mountain use, a practical starting point is usually:
- **Mild, low-elevation camping:** comfort rating around 5°C/40°F
- **General three-season mountain use:** comfort rating around 0°C/32°F
- **Cold sleepers or near-freezing forecasts:** comfort rating around –5°C/23°F

Use the **comfort rating** if you sleep cold. The “lower limit” is intended for a warmer sleeper and is not the same as guaranteed comfort. ISO/EN ratings are useful for comparing brands, but real-world warmth also depends on the sleeping pad, clothing, humidity, fatigue, nutrition, and individual metabolism. [citation:Understanding Sleeping Bag Temperature Ratings](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/understanding-sleeping-bag-temperature-ratings.html)

### Insulation choice
- **Down:** lighter, smaller packed size, longer service life when cared for properly.
- **Synthetic:** lower initial cost and more forgiving in very wet environments.

### Brand shortlist
- **Value:** Decathlon/Forclaz, Kelty, Marmot
- **Balanced:** Nemo, Sea to Summit, Rab, Mountain Equipment
- **Premium:** Western Mountaineering, Feathered Friends

## Sleeping pad
- [ ] Insulated inflatable pad
- [ ] ASTM-rated R-value around **3–4 for ordinary summer use**
- [ ] R-value around **4–5+ for cold ground, high elevations, or shoulder seasons**
- [ ] Repair patches
- [ ] Inflation sack if supplied
- [ ] Optional thin closed-cell foam pad as backup and camp seat

The sleeping bag cannot perform properly if the ground is drawing heat through an inadequately insulated pad.

Recommended brands:
- **Value:** Klymit, Decathlon, Naturehike

- **Premium/light:** Therm-a-Rest, Exped

# 7. Water storage and treatment

- [ ] Capacity to carry at least **2–3 L**
- [ ] Durable water bottle or hydration reservoir
- [ ] Lightweight squeeze or gravity filter
- [ ] Dedicated dirty-water container
- [ ] Chemical purification tablets as backup
- [ ] Electrolyte packets for hot, strenuous days
- [ ] Filter backflushing or cleaning accessory
- [ ] Insulating bottle sleeve for freezing conditions, if relevant

### Recommended filter types

For solo or two-person trips:
- Squeeze filter: light, simple, inexpensive
- Soft-flask filter: fast access but less durable container
- Gravity filter: excellent for groups and camp use

Good brands include **Sawyer, Katadyn, Platypus, MSR, and LifeStraw**.

Do not assume clear mountain water is safe. The CDC advises that boiling is the most reliable method for killing waterborne germs; the next-best approach is filtration followed by disinfection. Many portable filters remove parasites and bacteria but not viruses, so treatment must match local contamination risks. [citation:CDC Water Treatment for Hiking and Camping](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/prevention/water-treatment-hiking-camping-traveling.html)

CDC boiling guidance is:
- Rolling boil for **one minute**
- Rolling boil for **three minutes above 6,500 ft/1,980 m**
[citation:CDC Water Treatment for Hiking and Camping](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/prevention/water-treatment-hiking-camping-traveling.html)

A practical combination is a Sawyer Squeeze or Katadyn BeFree plus individually packaged chlorine-dioxide tablets. Protect hollow-fiber filters from freezing; once frozen while wet, internal damage may be impossible to verify.

# 13. Mountaineering-specific equipment

Do **not** assume normal hiking gear is sufficient for mountaineering. Exact equipment depends on whether the route involves snow, glaciers, rock climbing, avalanche terrain, or merely nontechnical alpine hiking.

## Personal technical equipment
- [ ] Certified climbing helmet
- [ ] Mountaineering harness
- [ ] Locking carabiners
- [ ] Appropriate mountaineering boots
- [ ] Crampons compatible with those boots
- [ ] Ice axe of appropriate type and length
- [ ] Waterproof gaiters
- [ ] Climbing gloves plus dry spare pair
- [ ] Glacier glasses and backup eye protection
- [ ] Technical shell clothing
- [ ] Emergency bivy
- [ ] Satellite communicator

## Glacier travel equipment
- [ ] Climbing rope
- [ ] Prusik loops or mechanical ascenders
- [ ] Pulleys
- [ ] Additional carabiners
- [ ] Snow anchors or ice screws as the route requires
- [ ] Crevasse-rescue equipment

## Avalanche terrain
- [ ] Avalanche transceiver
- [ ] Metal-bladed shovel
- [ ] Probe
- [ ] Formal avalanche education
- [ ] Daily avalanche forecast and terrain plan

Buying these items without learning their use creates false confidence. For your first trips, rent the helmet, harness, boots, crampons, axe, rope, and avalanche equipment through a guide service or mountaineering school. Brands worth considering after training include **Petzl, Black Diamond, Grivel, CAMP, Mammut, Blue Ice, Edelrid, and DMM**.

Crampons must match both the route and boot interface. Flexible hiking footwear may not safely accept semi-automatic or automatic crampons. Have the complete boot-and-crampon combination inspected and fitted by a specialist.

- Backpacks

# 18. Final pre-trip checklist

Before every departure:

- [ ] Check hourly weather at trailhead and summit elevation
- [ ] Check fire, wildlife, permit, food-storage, and waste rules
- [ ] Download offline maps
- [ ] Carry a physical map and compass
- [ ] Leave route, vehicle, return time, and emergency instructions with someone
- [ ] Inspect tent, pad, stove, filter, headlamp, and batteries
- [ ] Confirm sleeping system is adequate for the forecast low
- [ ] Carry one extra day of food
- [ ] Carry water treatment plus a backup
- [ ] Pack insulation for the worst credible conditions, not the parking-lot weather
- [ ] Set a turnaround time
- [ ] Be prepared to cancel or retreat

Test the complete system on a local overnight trip close to an easy exit before taking it into remote terrain. Pack and unpack it at home, pitch the tent in rain or wind if possible, use the stove, treat water, navigate in airplane mode, and walk several hours with the expected load.
