# Modular, Beginner-Appropriate Gear System for Summer Sichuan–Tibet Trekking and Shanghai Shakedowns

**Status date:** 12 September 2026  
**Currency:** RMB  
**Scope:** Summer, nontechnical trekking and camping—no glacier travel, ropes, technical rock climbing, or technical ice climbing.

## Executive recommendation

Do not buy one rigid “all-weather mountaineering set.” Build a **modular three-season trekking system** around:

1. A **properly fitted 50–60 L framed backpack** for self-supported trips, plus an optional 18–30 L daypack.
2. A **waterproof pack liner**; add a rain cover only for convenience.
3. Synthetic or merino hiking layers, a light fleece, a separate insulated jacket, a wind-resistant layer, a **fully seam-taped three-layer rain shell**, and rain pants.
4. Trail shoes or light hiking boots selected by actual terrain and fit—not by the word “mountaineering.”
5. Two trekking poles.
6. A double-wall, three-season tent in the **1.5–2.3 kg packed-weight range**, unless a guide supplies shelter.
7. An **ISO 23537-rated sleeping bag** and an **ASTM F3340-rated sleeping pad**:
   - Milder baseline: approximately **0°C Comfort bag** and **R 4–5 pad**.
   - Colder high camps: approximately **−5°C to −10°C Comfort bag** and **R 5–7 pad**, selected after obtaining a route forecast.
8. A reliable canister stove, 750–1,000 ml solo pot or 1.5–2.5 L group pot, local fuel, and two ignition methods.
9. Two independent water-treatment methods: normally **filter plus chlorine dioxide**, with boiling as another backup.
10. Phone navigation with two offline map systems, route GPX, compass, power bank, headlamp, whistle, emergency insulation, first aid, repair supplies, and—on remote routes—a **China-compatible rented or operator-supplied satellite/BeiDou communicator**.
11. Conservative acclimatization, weather judgment, route knowledge, insurance, and a real evacuation plan.

The most versatile price-performance system is usually **not ultralight**. A realistic self-supported base weight is about **10–14 kg** before food, water and fuel. Spending heavily to go below this is less useful for a beginner than spending on fit, reliable sleep insulation, rain protection, navigation, communications and training.

## 1. Important limitations and how to interpret the recommendations

### Verified facts versus planning judgments

The following are supported by primary or manufacturer sources:

- ISO 23537-1:2022 is the current standardized thermal-rating framework for sleeping bags designed for limit temperatures of −20°C and above.[1]
- Therm-a-Rest lists the regular NeoAir XLite NXT at **370 g, 7.6 cm thick and R 4.5**.[2]
- Naturehike lists the Cloud Up 2 as a three-season tent with approximately **1.38 kg minimum weight, 1.8 kg packed weight**, a PU3000 mm+ fly and PU4000–5000 mm floor components.[3]
- CDC altitude-ascent and illness guidance, including gradual sleeping-altitude increases and the importance of the first three to five days of acclimatization.[4]
- CDC backcountry water-treatment and boiling guidance.[6]
- Regional weather, closure, permit and transport rules must be checked through the relevant official authorities.[9]–[16]

The product prices, system weights and service lives below are **planning ranges**, not fixed quotations or guarantees. Chinese retail prices vary substantially with:

- 618, 11.11 and brand-specific sales;
- exact model year;
- grey-market versus authorized import;
- domestic versus international warranty;
- counterfeit risk;
- size and color;
- whether a product’s advertised weight includes stakes, bags, footprint, repair kit or batteries.

Confirm the exact SKU on the manufacturer’s official site, then compare it with the listing from an authorized Chinese retailer.

## 2. Information to confirm before buying specialized gear

Because route and logistics remain undecided, the following questions must be answered before choosing the coldest sleeping bag, strongest tent, largest pack, traction device, satellite communicator or stove system.

### Route and environmental questions

Obtain from the park, guide, operator or local authority:

- Exact route, including alternates and evacuation exits.
- Trekking dates and normal seasonal opening period.
- Trailhead, finish point and road access.
- Maximum elevation.
- Highest **sleeping** elevation, which matters more for altitude illness than the highest daytime pass.
- Expected lowest campsite temperature and plausible cold-front minimum.
- Exposed ridge or pass sections.
- Snowfields, frozen mornings or verglas.
- Typical summer wind and whether camps are above tree line.
- Number and difficulty of stream crossings.
- Rain, mud, landslide, debris-flow and rockfall exposure.
- Availability of established campsites, huts, villages and resupply.
- Water-source frequency and known livestock contamination.
- Whether the route passes through a nature reserve, core protected zone, border area or military-sensitive area.
- Whether camping, independent through-trekking, stoves or open flames are allowed.

Use the [China Meteorological Administration][9], [China Weather][10], provincial authorities and the operator’s local observations. Forecasts for a town or county seat are not necessarily representative of a pass 1,500 m higher.

### Permit and access questions

Confirm directly with the relevant authority or licensed operator:

- Whether the route lies in Sichuan, the Tibet Autonomous Region, or crosses both.
- Whether nationality, passport type or visa category changes access requirements.
- Tibet Travel Permit and any Alien’s Travel, border-area, military-area or mountaineering permissions.
- Whether a licensed guide or organized tour is compulsory.
- Whether independent camping is permitted.
- Park booking, registration, reporting and daily-entry procedures.
- Temporary closures caused by weather, wildfire, construction or conservation.
- Whether itinerary changes invalidate permits.

Do not assume that rules for Tibetan prefectures in Sichuan are identical to rules inside the Tibet Autonomous Region. Check the [Sichuan Department of Culture and Tourism][11], [Ganzi government][12], [Ganzi emergency authority][13], [Aba government][14] and the exact park or scenic-area administration.

### Operator and shared-equipment questions

Before buying a tent, stove, large pack or satellite device, get a written equipment list showing:

- Tent make, capacity and occupants per tent.
- Whether tents are carried by clients, porters, pack animals or vehicles.
- Sleeping bag and pad specifications, cleanliness and availability.
- Cook system, fuel type and who supplies fuel.
- Meals, snacks and resupply.
- Water-treatment method.
- Group shelter.
- First-aid and oxygen equipment.
- Satellite or BeiDou communications.
- Guide qualifications and guide-to-client ratio.
- Porter/pack-animal weight allowance and waterproof duffel requirements.
- Personal daypack requirements.
- Emergency vehicle access and evacuation procedure.
- Rescue and evacuation insurance requirements.
- What happens if roads, parks or passes close.

A guide should not merely say “a warm sleeping bag.” Ask for the **ISO Comfort temperature**, pad R-value, tent occupancy and actual baggage limit.

### Transport questions

Fuel and cutting tools are major compatibility issues:

- Do not take gas canisters, petrol, alcohol or other stove fuel to a railway station or airport without checking current rules.
- Confirm knife and multitool restrictions with the airline, railway, metro and scenic area.
- Check power-bank labeling and capacity requirements with the actual airline. For Chinese domestic air travel, use a clearly marked compliant power bank and reconfirm current CAAC/airline policy.
- Arrange to buy compatible fuel near the destination or have the operator supply it.
- Check the official [China Railway 12306][15] prohibited-item information immediately before travel.

### Communications questions

Before purchasing a satellite communicator:

- Is the device legal to import, possess and operate in the intended region?
- Can a mainland-China account and subscription be activated?
- Is two-way messaging functional there?
- Which monitoring center receives SOS messages?
- Can it coordinate effectively with Chinese local rescue authorities?
- Does the operator instead provide a registered domestic BeiDou terminal?
- Does the route have China Mobile, China Telecom or China Unicom coverage?
- Are there valleys with no line of sight to satellites?

Until these questions are resolved, renting the operator’s known-working unit is more sensible than buying an inReach or foreign PLB. Garmin’s official product page identifies the inReach Mini 2 as a satellite communicator, but China-specific service and regulatory compatibility must be verified separately.[17]

## 3. Modular system architecture

### Module A — Universal core

Carry this on every meaningful hike, adjusted for trip length:

- Navigation: phone, offline maps, route file, simple compass.
- Illumination: headlamp.
- Sun protection: hat, sunglasses, sunscreen.
- Rain protection.
- Extra warmth appropriate to the forecast.
- Water and treatment method.
- Food plus emergency reserve.
- First aid, blister supplies and personal medication.
- Whistle.
- Emergency insulation.
- Knife-free repair kit if transport rules are uncertain.
- ID, permits, cash and emergency contacts.
- Power and cable.
- Toilet and hygiene supplies.
- Trekking poles where terrain warrants them.

### Module B — Overnight system

Add:

- Shelter and stakes.
- Sleeping bag/quilt.
- Insulated pad and repair kit.
- Stove, fuel and cookware if not supplied.
- Food storage.
- Camp clothing.
- Sanitation supplies.
- Larger backpack.

### Module C — Cold, windy high-camp system

Add or substitute:

- Warmer ISO-rated bag.
- Higher-R pad or foam-pad supplement.
- Hooded insulated jacket.
- Warm hat, neck gaiter and insulated gloves.
- Dry sleep base layers.
- More wind-stable tent and stronger stakes.
- Additional guy lines.
- Insulated water-bottle sleeve if sustained freezing is likely.
- Larger fuel margin.

### Module D — Remote and communications system

Add:

- Satellite or BeiDou messenger known to work locally.
- Larger power reserve.
- Paper map and route notes.
- More comprehensive first-aid and repair supplies.
- Emergency bivy.
- Additional food reserve.
- Group shelter where appropriate.
- Written check-in and overdue plan.

### Module E — Route-specific snow or ice system

Do not buy until the guide confirms need:

- Flexible microspikes for low-angle frozen paths.
- Lightweight gaiters.
- Helmet for a documented rockfall area.
- Snow baskets for poles.

Microspikes are not a substitute for crampons, an ice axe or skills on steep, hard snow. If an uncontrolled slip would have serious consequences, the route is no longer appropriate for an untrained nontechnical loadout.

### Items not to buy prematurely

- Mountaineering boots.
- Automatic or semi-automatic crampons.
- Ice axe.
- Climbing helmet unless the route has documented rockfall exposure.
- Harness, rope, carabiner rack or belay device.
- Avalanche equipment.
- Four-season expedition tent.
- −20°C expedition sleeping bag.
- Liquid-fuel expedition stove.
- Foreign satellite communicator with unconfirmed Chinese service.
- 70–90 L expedition pack.
- Large survival knife.

## 4. Backpack, liner and rain cover

### Recommended configuration

- **Day hiking:** 18–30 L.
- **Guided/porter-supported trekking:** 25–40 L daypack plus operator-specified duffel.
- **2–3-night self-supported trip:** generally 45–60 L.
- **Longer trip without resupply:** generally 55–70 L, but first reduce unnecessary gear rather than automatically buying 75 L.

A framed 50–60 L pack is the most adaptable first trekking pack. Choose 60–70 L only if carrying bulky synthetic insulation, group equipment, many days of food or a colder sleep system.

### Fit procedure

1. Measure torso length from the prominent C7 vertebra at the base of the neck to the line between the tops of the iliac crests.
2. Measure hip circumference around the iliac crest—not trouser waist.
3. Select the manufacturer’s size from its current chart.
4. Load the pack with **8–15 kg**, including bulky items.
5. Center the hip belt over the iliac crest and tighten it before shoulder straps.
6. Adjust shoulder straps, load lifters and sternum strap.
7. Walk, climb stairs and bend for at least 30–60 minutes.
8. Reject the pack if:
   - the hip belt slips;
   - shoulder straps leave a gap or dig into the neck;
   - most weight remains on the shoulders;
   - the frame hits the back of the head;
   - the pack sways;
   - pockets cannot be reached;
   - numbness or hot spots develop.

Do not buy a pack solely from height or body weight.

### Key purchasing criteria

- Adjustable torso length.
- Hip belt that wraps at least around the front half of the hips.
- Frame appropriate to expected load.
- Durable bottom fabric.
- Simple compression system.
- Accessible water pockets.
- Hip-belt pockets.
- Replaceable buckles where possible.
- Pack weight versus load-carrying comfort.

An ultralight 1.0–1.3 kg pack may work well at an 8–12 kg total load but become uncomfortable at 17–20 kg. A 1.8–2.4 kg framed pack can be better value for a beginner carrying conventional equipment.

### Liner versus cover

- A **40–70 L polyethylene compactor bag or purpose-made waterproof liner** is the primary protection for sleeping bag and dry clothing.
- Place critical items in individually sealed bags inside the liner.
- A pack cover helps keep the exterior less wet and muddy, but wind can remove it and water can enter around shoulder straps.
- In continuous rain, pack fabric and straps will still absorb water.

### Representative choices

| Level | Representative options | Approx. weight | Planning price | Best use |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Value | Decathlon Forclaz MT500 50+10; Naturehike/Kailas framed 50–60 L models | 1.6–2.2 kg | ¥700–1,400 | First self-supported pack; prioritize fit |
| Balanced light | Osprey Exos 58 | about 1.3 kg, size-dependent | ¥1,600–2,400 | Moderate loads and careful packing |
| Balanced durable | Osprey Kestrel 58; Gregory Zulu 55; Deuter Aircontact Lite 50+10 | 1.6–2.2 kg | ¥1,700–2,700 | Better load control and durability |
| Premium load carrier | Osprey Aether 55/65; Gregory Baltoro 65 | 2.1–2.6 kg | ¥2,400–3,700 | Heavy food, colder equipment or group loads |

### Field testing, care and service life

- Walk 10–15 km in Shanghai with 70%, then 100%, of intended load.
- Test in stairs, humidity and rain.
- Repack repeatedly until rain gear, water and first aid are accessible.
- After use, empty dirt, loosen straps and air-dry away from direct heat.
- Hand-clean with mild soap; do not machine-wash unless the manual explicitly allows it.
- A well-made pack can last approximately **7–15 years** of recreational use; mesh, coatings and zippers usually fail before the frame.

### Beginner mistakes

- Buying too large and filling it.
- Choosing by advertised liters without trying the correct torso size.
- Hanging heavy items on the outside.
- Carrying the sleeping bag unprotected.
- Assuming a pack cover makes the contents waterproof.
- Buying an ultralight frameless pack before reducing load.

## 5. Clothing system

## Base and hiking layers

### Recommended system

- 1–2 synthetic or merino short- or long-sleeve tops.
- Lightweight synthetic hiking trousers.
- Synthetic underwear.
- Optional lightweight shorts for Shanghai.
- One dry sleep/camp base-layer set kept sealed.

Avoid cotton for high-altitude trekking because it dries slowly and loses comfort when wet.

### Material trade-offs

- **Synthetic:** economical, fast-drying, durable, but retains odor.
- **Merino blend:** better odor control and broad comfort range, but more expensive and less abrasion-resistant.
- **Pure ultralight merino:** comfortable but poor value under pack straps.

A UPF 50+ sun hoodie can reduce sunscreen use and works well in exposed high-UV conditions. Look for a stated UPF test such as GB/T 18830 or a recognized equivalent.

## Active insulation

Use a 150–250-weight fleece or light synthetic active-insulation jacket. Fleece is inexpensive, durable and still useful when damp. This is a category where economical choices are generally adequate.

## Static insulation

Carry a separate insulated jacket for stops and camp:

- **Mild high-altitude summer:** lightweight hooded down or synthetic jacket.
- **Colder camps near or below freezing:** a warmer hooded jacket with meaningful fill weight, not merely a fashionable “800-fill” label.
- **Very wet itinerary:** synthetic insulation tolerates repeated dampness better.
- **Cold and mostly dry:** down has the best warmth-to-weight ratio.

Fill power measures loft quality, not total warmth. A jacket containing little high-fill-power down may be colder than a heavier jacket using lower-fill-power down.

## Wind protection

A 90–180 g wind shell is highly useful in dry wind and can prevent unnecessary wear on an expensive waterproof shell. It is optional if the rain shell breathes well and pack weight is constrained.

## Waterproof shell

### Purchasing criteria

Prefer:

- Three-layer construction for repeated backpack use.
- Fully taped seams.
- Adjustable hood with good visibility.
- Pit zips or generous mechanical ventilation.
- Cuffs that close over gloves.
- Hem that remains covered when reaching overhead.
- Room for fleece and insulated jacket.
- Fabric sturdy enough under shoulder straps.

Hydrostatic-head and breathability numbers are useful only when test methods are comparable. “20,000 mm/20,000 g” alone does not establish real comfort. Ventilation, seam construction, face-fabric durability and fit matter more.

A poncho is useful in hot, sheltered Shanghai rain, but is a poor primary shell for exposed, windy high-altitude passes.

## Rain pants

Look for:

- Full seam taping.
- Ankle zips large enough to pass over footwear.
- Reinforced lower legs if the route is muddy or brushy.
- Side zips for ventilation in premium models.
- Fit that permits a deep step and squat.

Very thin 100–180 g rain pants save weight but tear more easily. A 200–350 g pair is a better beginner compromise.

## Warm accessories and spare clothing

Carry according to forecast:

- Brimmed sun hat.
- Warm beanie covering the ears.
- Neck gaiter.
- Thin liner gloves.
- Waterproof shell mitts or gloves.
- Insulated gloves for colder camps.
- One dry pair of sleep socks.
- Spare underwear.
- Optional light camp footwear.

### Fit test

Try the complete system together:

1. Base layer.
2. Fleece.
3. Insulated jacket.
4. Rain shell.
5. Backpack.

Raise arms, rotate shoulders, squat, climb stairs and use poles. The shell must not compress the insulation or expose the lower back. Hoods must turn with the head without covering peripheral vision.

### Shanghai tests

- Walk for two hours in warm rain with the loaded pack.
- Determine whether internal moisture is sweat or leakage.
- Hose-test the hood, shoulders, zipper and seams.
- Practice adding layers without exposing dry clothing.
- Wear rain pants over muddy footwear.
- Confirm that shell cuffs, hood and hem remain functional in wind.

### Care and service life

- Wash waterproof clothing according to the membrane manufacturer’s instructions; body oils can reduce performance.
- Use technical detergent, rinse thoroughly and restore DWR with approved heat or treatment.
- Do not use ordinary fabric softener.
- Store clean and dry, loosely hung.
- Inspect for seam-tape peeling and internal delamination.

Planning life under recreational use:

- Base/fleece: 5–10 years.
- Down jacket: 8–15 years if stored uncompressed.
- Synthetic jacket: approximately 4–8 years before significant loft loss.
- Waterproof shell: often 3–7 years, depending heavily on abrasion, body oils and coating quality.

## 6. Footwear, socks, gaiters, poles and traction

## Footwear decision framework

### Breathable trail shoes

Best for:

- Hot and humid Shanghai hikes.
- Good trails.
- Fast drying.
- Light loads.
- Hikers with conditioned feet and ankles.

Limitations:

- Less protection in prolonged mud, cold rain and sharp scree.
- Water enters easily, although it also drains quickly.

### Waterproof low or mid hiking shoes

Best for:

- Cold, wet grass.
- Mud and shallow puddles.
- Long wet days around freezing.
- Moderate loads.

Limitations:

- Hotter in Shanghai.
- Once flooded from above, membrane footwear dries slowly.
- Membranes eventually leak or wear.

### Supportive mid boots

Choose when:

- Carrying a heavy load.
- Terrain is rough and rocky.
- The operator specifically recommends ankle and underfoot protection.
- The hiker has demonstrated benefit from a stiffer platform.

A boot does not prevent all ankle injuries. Fit, fatigue, poles and movement skills matter more than boot height alone.

### Fit procedure

Try footwear late in the day with intended socks:

- Leave approximately 10–15 mm of space ahead of the longest toe.
- Toes must not hit the front on a steep descent.
- Heel lift should be minimal.
- The forefoot must not be compressed.
- No pressure on bunions, ankle bones or Achilles tendon.
- Orthotics or insoles must be tested in the actual shoe.
- Walk down a ramp or stairs for at least 20 minutes.
- Do not assume a larger size fixes an unsuitable last.

Break in over at least 30–80 km before a major trip. Trail shoes need less structural break-in than leather boots, but feet still need adaptation.

### Representative footwear

| Level | Examples | Approx. price | Comments |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Value | Decathlon MH500/MH900; Kailas hiking models | ¥400–1,000 | Strong value if the last fits |
| Balanced | Salomon X Ultra 4/5, Merrell Moab, La Sportiva Spire | ¥900–1,700 | Broad availability; counterfeit risk on marketplaces |
| Supportive/premium | Salomon Quest, Lowa Renegade, Scarpa trekking models | ¥1,500–2,800 | More structure and durability, but heavier and hotter |

Pair weight varies considerably with size; fit is more important than a 100–200 g difference.

## Socks

Use:

- Synthetic or merino-blend trekking socks.
- A close, wrinkle-free fit.
- Two hiking pairs plus one dry sleeping pair for most short trips.

Thin liner socks help some hikers but create more sliding for others. Test them.

Replace socks when cushioning becomes flat or holes and hard seams develop. Typical life is roughly 300–800 hiking km, highly variable.

## Gaiters

- Low gaiters: dust, gravel and light mud.
- Mid/high waterproof gaiters: deep mud, wet vegetation or shallow snow.
- Not universally required.

## Trekking poles

Two poles improve stability, distribute effort and help probe mud or water. Look for:

- Reliable external lever locks.
- Replaceable carbide tips.
- Correct extended length.
- Comfortable grips.
- Moderate weight, approximately 400–550 g per pair for value models.
- Snow baskets only if required.

Value: Decathlon or Naturehike, approximately ¥200–500.  
Balanced: Black Diamond Trail series, approximately ¥600–1,000.  
Premium: Leki, approximately ¥900–1,800.

Do not place both hands through straps during a dangerous stream crossing where poles could trap the arms.

## Traction

Carry flexible microspikes only if the operator reports:

- Frozen trails.
- Low-angle hard snow.
- Icy boardwalks or passes where a slip can be arrested safely.

Do not use microspikes to justify crossing steep snow above cliffs or rivers. That requires different skills, route choice or a guide.

## 7. Shelter, footprint and stakes

## General recommendation

For a beginner who may camp independently, choose a **double-wall, semi-freestanding or freestanding two-person three-season tent** with:

- Full-coverage fly.
- Good ventilation.
- Proven seam sealing.
- At least one useful vestibule.
- Aluminum poles.
- Adequate guy-out points.
- Packed weight approximately 1.5–2.3 kg.
- Enough floor length for the user, sleeping bag and thick pad.

A two-person tent is comfortable for one person and manageable for two. Check floor dimensions rather than relying on “2P.” Many two-person backpacking tents are narrow for two wide pads.

### Naturehike Cloud Up 2

The official Cloud Up 2 specifications include:

- Approximately 210 × 125 × 105 cm.
- Approximately 1.38 kg minimum and 1.8 kg packed.
- 20D silicone-coated nylon fly, PU3000 mm+.
- Aluminum poles and stakes.
- Double-wall construction.[3]

It is a strong value choice for sheltered three-season trips and Shanghai testing. Its limitations are a single front entrance, modest vestibule convenience and less structural margin than a heavier storm-oriented tent.

| Value | Naturehike Cloud Up 2 | 1.8 kg official packed weight | ¥700–1,200 | Good first tent; pitch carefully |
| Value, roomier | Naturehike Mongar 2; Mobi Garden lightweight 2P models | 1.8–2.4 kg | ¥900–1,700 | Two-door designs can be more livable |

### There is no universal tent wind rating

Hydrostatic head measures fabric water-pressure resistance, not complete weatherworthiness. Real performance also depends on:

- Pole geometry and diameter.
- Fabric stretch when wet.
- Seam sealing.
- Fly coverage.
- Stake holding.
- Number and placement of guy lines.
- Pitch orientation.
- Site selection.

Naturehike itself cautions that higher fabric ratings do not automatically produce better rain protection.[3]

### Footprint

A footprint is useful on:

- Abrasive gravel.
- Sharp vegetation.
- Repeated commercial campsite use.
- Mud where easier cleaning is valuable.

It is not always necessary on soft grass. It must remain entirely underneath the tent floor; exposed edges collect rainwater.

A cut-to-size polyethylene sheet is usually adequate. Avoid a heavy branded footprint unless abrasion is a recurring issue.

### Stakes

Carry stakes matched to the ground:

- Y/V aluminum stakes for mixed soil.
- Longer, wider stakes for soft alpine turf.
- Spare cord for rocks or deadman anchors.
- One or two spare stakes.

Do not assume the lightest included stakes will hold in wet, wind-exposed ground.

### Pitch and field tests

Practice until the tent can be fully pitched in darkness and rain:

1. Pitch in dry weather.
2. Pitch with gloves.
3. Pitch without placing the inner tent on wet ground for long.
4. Hose-test fly coverage and seam behavior.
5. Sleep in it during Shanghai rain and humidity.
6. Test ventilation with fly doors partly open where safe.
7. Retension after nylon absorbs moisture.
8. Practice using all guy lines.

Never cook inside the sleeping compartment or sealed vestibule. Carbon monoxide, fire and canister overheating can be fatal.

### Care and service life

Naturehike recommends clean water, a soft sponge and mild cleaner; it warns against bleach, harsh detergent, dry cleaning, machine washing and machine drying. Tents must be completely dry before storage and kept in a cool, dry place away from prolonged heat and sunlight.[3]

A frequently used lightweight tent may provide approximately 100–300 nights before substantial coating, zipper or fabric deterioration; careful recreational use may span 5–10 years or more. UV exposure and storage while damp shorten life dramatically.

## 8. Sleeping bag or quilt and insulated pad

## Sleeping-bag ratings

Buy according to **ISO 23537 Comfort and Limit ratings**, not vague labels such as “spring/autumn,” “extreme −10°C,” or fill weight alone.[1]

For a beginner:

- Use **Comfort** as the main decision value.
- The Limit value is more relevant to a warm sleeper in a curled posture.
- Extreme is a survival-related figure, not a temperature at which a beginner should plan to sleep.

Add safety margin for:

- Cold sleepers.
- Women using a unisex rating.
- Fatigue or inadequate food.
- Wind exposure.
- Damp insulation.
- High altitude.
- A pad that is less warm than expected.
- Forecast uncertainty.

### Provisional temperature bands

| Expected campsite minimum | Conservative bag choice |
|---|---|
| Consistently above 8°C | Comfort around 5°C |
| Around 2–8°C | Comfort around 0°C |
| Around −3 to 3°C | Comfort around −5°C |
| Around −8 to −3°C | Comfort around −10°C |

These are selection judgments, not guarantees. Obtain the route’s sleeping elevations and cold-front forecast before buying.

## Down versus synthetic

### Down

Advantages:

- Best warmth-to-weight and packed volume.
- Long service life if properly cared for.

Choose:

- Clearly stated fill weight.
- Fill power.
- Box-baffle construction for colder bags.
- Hydrophobic treatment only as a secondary feature.
- Responsible Down Standard or equivalent traceability when available.

Limitations:

- Expensive.
- Loses loft when soaked.
- Needs careful storage and drying.

### Synthetic

Advantages:

- Lower initial price.
- Better tolerance of dampness.
- Easier for very humid use.

Limitations:

- Heavier and bulkier.
- Loses loft faster from repeated compression.

## Quilts

Quilts save weight by eliminating insulation beneath the body, where it is compressed. They work best with:

- A properly sized insulated pad.
- Reliable attachment straps.
- A user accustomed to controlling drafts.

For a beginner expecting cold, windy camps, a mummy sleeping bag is usually easier and more forgiving.

## Size and fit

Measure:

- Standing height.
- Shoulder or chest circumference.
- Hip circumference.
- Foot size.
- Preferred sleep position.

Test while wearing dry base layers:

- Hood closes without restricting breathing.
- Toes do not compress the footbox.
- Shoulders do not compress insulation.
- Side sleepers can turn without opening drafts.
- The bag is not excessively large; dead air must still be warmed.

## Sleeping pad

Use an R-value tested to **ASTM F3340** where possible. R-values can generally be added when pads are stacked.

Provisional choices:

- Warm lowland summer: R 2–3.
- General high-altitude summer baseline: R 4–5.
- Freezing or colder high camps: R 5–7.
- Sustained severe cold: route-specific system beyond the default brief.

The regular NeoAir XLite NXT is an example of a premium lightweight pad: **370 g, R 4.5, 7.6 cm thick**, with pump sack, stuff sack and field repair kit included.[2]

A thin closed-cell foam pad under an inflatable:

- Adds approximately R 1.5–2 depending on model.
- Protects against puncture.
- Provides emergency insulation if the air pad fails.
- Adds bulk.

### Representative sleep options

| Category | Value | Balanced | Premium |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0°C-class bag | Black Ice, Mobi Garden, Decathlon bags with a published ISO Comfort figure; ¥600–1,500 | Sea to Summit, Rab, Mountain Hardwear; ¥1,500–3,000 | Western Mountaineering/Feathered Friends where legitimately imported; ¥4,000–7,000+ |
| Colder bag | Chinese down models with verified fill and ISO rating; ¥1,000–2,200 | Rab Neutrino/Alpine, Sea to Summit Spark/Ascent by rating; ¥2,200–4,000 | Premium high-fill down; ¥4,500–8,000 |
| Pad R 3–5 | Decathlon or Chinese insulated pads with independently stated test method; ¥400–900 | Sea to Summit or Nemo insulated models; ¥900–1,700 | Therm-a-Rest XLite NXT, typically ¥1,500–2,300 |
| Pad R 6–8 | Chinese cold pads only if rating method is credible; ¥700–1,200 | Therm-a-Rest XTherm-class pad; ¥1,900–2,800 | Premium comfort/wide versions; ¥2,300–3,500 |

Do not assume a Chinese pad’s advertised “R 5.8” is equivalent to ASTM F3340 unless the test method is explicitly documented.

### Field testing

- Inflate the pad and leave it weighted overnight for at least 24 hours.
- Mark suspected leaks with soapy water.
- Test the valve and repair kit.
- Sleep on the system in Shanghai during the coolest available weather.
- If possible, use a safe cold room or colder-region trip before relying on a new system below freezing.
- Test pad width: 51 cm is light but narrow; many side sleepers prefer 64 cm.
- Confirm that two chosen pads fit the tent floor.

### Care and service life

- Keep down bags loose in a large breathable storage sack.
- Never store bags compressed or damp.
- Wash infrequently with appropriate down/synthetic cleaner.
- Air after every trip.
- Use sleep clothing to reduce body-oil contamination.
- Protect inflatable pads from thorns and sharp tent-floor debris.

Therm-a-Rest advises storing NeoAir pads with the valve open and the pad rolled in its stuff sack, while self-inflating foam pads should be stored unrolled, fully inflated and dry with the valve open.[2]

Planning life:

- Quality down bag: 10–20 years or longer.
- Synthetic bag: 5–10 years.
- Air pad: approximately 5–10 years, with large variation.
- Closed-cell foam: long functional life, though compression reduces comfort.

## 9. Stove, fuel, cookware and food

## Recommended stove type

For most summer trips, use a screw-thread canister stove compatible with locally available threaded canisters.

### Solo or pair

- Upright canister stove.
- 750–1,000 ml pot.
- Wind-resistant burner.
- Stable four-arm pot support where possible.

### Group or colder use

- Remote-canister stove.
- 1.5–2.5 L pot.
- Wider base and greater stability.
- Only invert a canister if the stove is explicitly designed for liquid-feed operation.

### Representative options

| Level | Examples | Approx. stove weight | Planning price | Evaluation |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Economy | BRS-3000T | about 25 g | ¥100–180 | Very light; narrow support and weaker wind/stability margin |
| Value | Fire-Maple FMS-300T or comparable | about 45–70 g | ¥150–350 | Better first purchase than the lightest possible burner |
| Balanced | Soto WindMaster | about 87 g with four-leg support | ¥500–800 | Excellent wind performance and simmering |
| Balanced | MSR PocketRocket Deluxe | about 83 g | ¥600–950 | Refined upright stove; importer support varies |
| Group/cold | Fire-Maple or MSR remote-canister models | 130–350 g | ¥350–1,200 | More stable, route/fuel dependent |

A piezo igniter is convenient but not an emergency backup. Carry two mini lighters in different waterproof locations; waterproof matches are optional.

## Fuel compatibility and restrictions

- Verify valve type and thread.
- Do not use an adapter merely because it physically connects; pressure and support may be unsafe.
- Cold reduces canister pressure. Keep the canister above snow, warm it with body heat before use, and never expose it to flame or excessive heat.
- Never surround an upright canister with a full windscreen; reflected heat can overheat the canister.
- Buy fuel locally or through the operator.
- Reconfirm train and flight restrictions before every trip.

## Cookware

Economical aluminum cookware is adequate:

- Aluminum spreads heat well and costs less.
- Titanium saves weight but creates hot spots and provides little safety improvement.
- Stainless steel is durable but heavy.

Suggested sizes:

- Solo boiling meals: 750–900 ml.
- Solo real cooking: 1.0–1.3 L.
- Two people: 1.3–1.8 L.
- Three to four people: 2.0–3.0 L.

Carry:

- Lid.
- Spoon.
- Cup only if not drinking from pot.
- Small cleaning cloth.
- Minimal biodegradable soap where permitted.
- Stove base or canister stand for stability.

## Fuel planning

Fuel use varies too much for a universal figure. Test the exact stove, pot and meals in wind. Record fuel mass before and after:

- Boiling one liter.
- Cooking breakfast and dinner.
- Treating emergency water.

Add margin for cold, wind, altitude, inefficient group cooking and emergency boiling. Ask the operator whether hot water is supplied.

## Food storage

- Use odor-resistant bags and keep food organized.
- Do not leave food scraps.
- Protect food from rodents and livestock.
- Ask local staff whether bears or other food-conditioned wildlife require canisters, hangs or designated storage.
- Do not assume imported bear-canister practices apply everywhere; follow local regulations.
- Keep food outside the sleeping area where animal risk and campsite rules require it.

## 10. Navigation, power, lighting and communications

## Navigation system

Use three levels:

1. **Primary:** phone with offline map and route GPX.
2. **Independent backup:** second app or second group phone containing the same route.
3. **Non-electronic:** paper route overview, written waypoints and baseplate compass.

Possible apps include AMap/Gaode for roads and services plus a separate offline topographic or GPX-capable app. Verify every imported route because Chinese map systems can use different coordinate systems, producing visible offsets between WGS84 GPS coordinates and GCJ-02 map layers.

### Navigation preparation

- Obtain the GPX from the operator or authoritative route source.
- Verify start, camps, water, passes, junctions and evacuation exits.
- Add roadheads, clinics and ranger/park offices.
- Download maps at multiple zoom levels.
- Put the phone in airplane mode and confirm GNSS still functions.
- Practice navigating in Shanghai without mobile data.
- Learn to take and follow a compass bearing.

A phone is not waterproof merely because it has a high IP rating. Use a protective case or sealed bag.

## Power

Approximate planning:

- Day hike: phone’s internal battery may suffice; 5,000–10,000 mAh backup.
- 2–3 nights: 10,000–20,000 mAh.
- Longer remote trip: 20,000 mAh or more, depending on photography and communications.
- Solar charging is usually poor value for short trips and unreliable in rain; it becomes useful only on long, exposed itineraries.

Carry:

- Short, durable cables.
- Correct charging adapters.
- A spare cable.
- Waterproof storage.
- Power-bank identification and compliant labeling for air travel.

Keep batteries inside insulation in cold conditions.

## Headlamp

Criteria:

- Regulated useful output, not only maximum lumens.
- Low mode around camp.
- Red mode optional.
- Physical lockout.
- Glove-friendly switch.
- At least IPX4; IP67-class protection is preferable in sustained rain.
- Replaceable batteries or reliable USB-C charging.
- Published ANSI/PLATO FL 1 performance where available.

Representative options:

- Nitecore NU25-class lamps: about 45–60 g, ¥200–350.
- Fenix HM50R-class lamps: about 75–85 g, ¥350–550.
- Petzl Actik Core: about 85–90 g, ¥450–700.

Carry spare power. A rechargeable headlamp without a power bank is not redundant.

## Satellite and emergency communication

Mobile coverage should never be the sole remote emergency plan. A satellite or BeiDou device can fail because of:

- Subscription lapse.
- Regulatory or account restrictions.
- Poor sky view in deep valleys.
- Battery depletion.
- Antenna orientation.
- Delayed monitoring-center coordination.
- Inability to communicate the exact local place name.
- No nearby rescue resource despite successful messaging.

A PLB normally offers one-way distress signaling and lacks routine two-way communication. A messenger can permit check-ins and details, but only if its network and response arrangements work legally and operationally in China.

For an undecided route, **renting an operator-provided, field-proven BeiDou or satellite unit is preferable**.

Create a written communications plan:

- Routine check-in time.
- Missed-check-in threshold.
- Overdue threshold.
- Local emergency contacts.
- Who holds permit and insurance information.
- What exact message means “delayed but safe.”
- What exact message means “initiate rescue.”

## 11. Water carrying and treatment

## Capacity

Typical carrying capacity:

- Water-rich route: 1.5–2 L.
- Dry exposed stage: 3–4 L or operator-specified capacity.
- Camp without nearby water: additional collapsible bag.

Use two containers so one puncture does not remove all capacity.

## Treatment hierarchy

### Hollow-fiber filter

Good for bacteria and protozoa when used within its stated claims. It generally does **not** remove:

- Viruses reliably.
- Dissolved chemicals.
- Heavy metals.
- Salt.
- Agricultural or industrial pollutants.

Examples include Sawyer Squeeze and Katadyn BeFree. Typical prices are approximately ¥350–650, but counterfeit filters are a serious marketplace concern.

Critical limitation: a wet hollow-fiber filter can be permanently damaged by freezing, often without visible evidence. Sleep with it inside the sleeping bag or replace it after suspected freezing.

### Chemical treatment

Chlorine dioxide tablets or drops are a lightweight backup and can improve protection against some organisms not reliably handled by basic filters. Cold or turbid water may require a longer contact time; follow the exact product label.

### Boiling

CDC guidance is to bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute and, above 6,500 ft/approximately 1,980 m, boil for three minutes.[6] Boiling requires sufficient fuel and does not remove chemical contamination.

### Purifier

A device such as GRAYL may address a broader organism range than a basic hollow-fiber filter, but it is heavier, more expensive and cartridge-limited. Verify its current testing and claims rather than relying on the word “purifier.”

## Best practical system

For most routes:

- Collect from the cleanest available flowing source.
- Prefilter sediment through cloth if needed.
- Filter.
- Add chemical treatment where human or livestock contamination creates viral concern.
- Keep boiling as a backup.

Avoid collecting immediately downstream of camps, settlements or livestock concentrations.

## Field test and maintenance

- Treat several liters in Shanghai before departure.
- Learn backflushing or cartridge cleaning.
- Measure actual flow.
- Label dirty and clean containers.
- Never let the dirty-side hose touch the clean bottle opening.
- Disinfect and dry according to the manual after trips.
- Replace cartridges by manufacturer life, reduced flow, damage or suspected freezing—not merely by age.

The CDC’s official backcountry guide provides the primary treatment framework.[6] EPA emergency-disinfection guidance is useful as a backup reference but does not replace the product’s own label.[7]

## 12. First aid, medications, hygiene and sanitation

## Personal first-aid kit

Carry supplies you know how to use:

- Adhesive dressings.
- Sterile gauze.
- Medical tape.
- Elastic wrap.
- Blister patches or hydrocolloid dressing.
- Leukotape or equivalent friction tape.
- Antiseptic wipes.
- Small irrigation syringe.
- Tweezers.
- Nitrile gloves.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- Personal pain medication.
- Antihistamine if medically appropriate.
- Anti-diarrheal medication under medical guidance.
- Personal prescriptions.
- Copies of prescriptions and allergy information.

For remote groups, add shared wound, splinting, hypothermia and evacuation supplies under the guide’s medical plan.

Do not carry prescription altitude medication without medical consultation. Acetazolamide and dexamethasone have indications, contraindications and side effects. CDC provides risk-category guidance, but it is not individualized prescribing advice.[4]

## Blister prevention

- Trim nails.
- Keep socks wrinkle-free.
- Stop at the first hot spot.
- Dry or change socks.
- Use tape before a blister forms.
- Do not puncture an intact blister unless necessary and trained to manage it.
- Carry footwear that has already passed loaded downhill tests.

## Hygiene

- Hand sanitizer.
- Small soap supply.
- Quick-dry towel.
- Toothbrush.
- Menstrual supplies if applicable.
- Unscented wipes, packed out.
- Toilet paper in a waterproof bag.
- Waste bag.

## Sanitation

Follow local park rules first. Where catholes are permitted, standard Leave No Trace practice is to remain well away from water, trails and camps and pack out toilet paper; some alpine, desert, frozen or heavily used areas require all solid waste to be carried out.[8]

Carry:

- Trowel.
- Opaque waste bags.
- Sealable inner bags.
- Hand sanitizer.

Do not assume burial is legal or environmentally suitable at high elevation.

## Sun and insect protection

High altitude increases UV exposure. Carry:

- Category 3 or route-appropriate UV-blocking sunglasses.
- Spare prescription glasses if needed.
- UPF hat and clothing.
- Broad-spectrum SPF 30–50+ sunscreen.
- SPF lip balm.
- Insect repellent containing an established active ingredient such as DEET or icaridin/picaridin.
- Head net only where insect reports justify it.

Reapply sunscreen; a high SPF does not last all day through sweat and abrasion.

- Rodents.

## 15. High-altitude, storms, rain, streams and rapid weather changes

## Acclimatization

CDC explains that the first three to five days after ascent are especially important. Above approximately 3,000 m, a conservative guideline is to increase sleeping elevation by no more than about **500 m per night**, adding an acclimatization night for every 1,000 m of sleeping-elevation gain.[4]

CDC also recommends avoiding a direct ascent from below 1,200 m to a sleeping elevation above approximately 2,750 m in one day where possible. Two to three nights around 2,450–2,750 m before going higher are protective.[4]

Fitness does not prevent acute mountain sickness.

## Acute mountain sickness

Typical AMS pattern:

- Recent ascent.
- Headache.
- One or more of nausea, appetite loss, dizziness or unusual fatigue.
- Often beginning 2–12 hours after arrival or after the first night.[4]

Rules:

- Do not ascend to sleep higher while symptomatic.
- Rest, hydrate normally and monitor.
- If symptoms worsen, descend.
- Improvement after descending at least approximately 300 m supports the diagnosis.
- Do not let itinerary pressure override symptoms.

## Emergency warning signs

### Suspected HACE

- Ataxia or inability to walk heel-to-toe.
- Confusion.
- Marked drowsiness.
- Irrational behavior.
- Altered consciousness.

### Suspected HAPE

- Breathlessness at rest.
- Rapid decline in exercise capacity.
- Cough, especially worsening.
- Chest tightness.
- Blue/grey lips or severe weakness.

These require immediate descent and urgent medical/rescue action. Supplemental oxygen can help where available, but it must not delay evacuation.

A pulse oximeter can provide context, but consumer units are affected by cold fingers, motion, poor circulation and device quality. A reassuring number does not overrule severe symptoms.

The UIAA medical papers include field guidance on AMS, HACE, HAPE and evaluating commercial trekking operations.[5]

## Turnaround and evacuation planning

Before departure, establish:

- Fixed turnaround time.
- Weather turnaround criteria.
- Maximum acceptable stream level.
- Minimum visibility.
- Who can order descent.
- Nearest lower camp.
- Road and vehicle evacuation points.
- Bailout trails.
- Emergency contacts.
- Insurance authorization procedure.
- Who remains with an ill person.
- Who carries shelter, communications and first aid.

A group must never split in a way that leaves either subgroup without navigation, shelter or communication.

## Rain, landslides and debris flow

Sichuan and Tibet can experience rain-triggered geological hazards; September 2026 warnings included parts of both regions.[10] Avoid:

- Narrow gullies during or after heavy rain.
- Camping in dry riverbeds.
- Fresh landslide deposits.
- Unstable slopes beneath cliffs.
- River terraces showing recent flood debris.
- Road cuts during intense rainfall.

A clear sky at camp does not remove upstream flash-flood risk.

## Lightning

- Leave ridges, summits and exposed passes early.
- Avoid isolated trees, metal fences and water edges.
- Spread group members apart while maintaining contact.
- Do not shelter in a shallow cave or directly beneath an overhang where current can bridge.
- Wait well after the last thunder before returning to exposed terrain.

## Stream crossings

- Cross early when water may be lower.
- Scout upstream and downstream.
- Avoid crossing immediately above waterfalls or rapids.
- Use poles.
- Loosen or unbuckle the pack belt only if doing so will not destabilize the person.
- Wear secure footwear; do not cross barefoot.
- Turn around if depth, speed, temperature or downstream consequence is unacceptable.

No piece of gear makes a dangerous flood crossing safe.

## 17. Sales, rentals and second-hand equipment

## Good items to buy on sale

- Clothing after fit test.
- Packs from a known model year.
- Tents with full warranty.
- Stoves from authorized retailers.
- Trekking poles.
- Headlamps.
- Cookware.

A 30% discount on the wrong size or obsolete membrane is not value.

## Best items to rent or borrow

- Colder −10°C or expedition sleeping bag.
- Strong storm-oriented tent.
- Large 65–75 L pack.
- Satellite or BeiDou communicator.
- Helmet.
- Microspikes.
- Group stove and large cookware.
- Bear-resistant storage if specifically required.
- Porter duffel.

## Better purchased new

- Footwear, unless nearly unused and perfectly fitting.
- Hollow-fiber water filter.
- Personal medication and first-aid consumables.
- Underwear and hygiene items.
- Emergency communication electronics when battery and account history cannot be verified.
- Inflatable pad if used condition is uncertain.
- Gas hose/regulator components with corrosion or undocumented repairs.
- Safety-critical climbing gear—not needed for this brief.

## Used-equipment inspection

### Tent

- Pitch it fully.
- Smell for mildew or polyurethane breakdown.
- Inspect seam tape, floor tackiness, fly delamination and UV fading.
- Flex poles and inspect ferrules, shock cord and cracks.
- Check every zipper and guy point.
- Hose-test.
- Confirm all stakes and pole sections.

### Sleeping bag

- Confirm model and standardized rating.
- Compare loft with manufacturer photos/specifications.
- Check cold spots, clumping, odors and stains.
- Inspect baffles, zipper and drawcords.
- Verify it was stored loose and dry.
- Budget for professional cleaning.

### Sleeping pad

- Inflate for 24 hours under load.
- Test valve.
- Inspect for bubbles, delamination and mold.
- Confirm repair patches adhere.
- Avoid pads with large bubbles or separating internal baffles.

### Backpack

- Load heavily.
- Inspect frame, hip-belt foam, shoulder attachment, buckle cracks, coating and zipper teeth.
- Check whether the correct torso size can still be adjusted.

### Stove

- Inspect threads, seals, burner deformation and corrosion.
- Test outdoors with soapy water at connections.
- Reject unauthorized modifications or damaged hoses.

### Footwear

- Check midsole compression, sole separation, uneven wear, cracked membrane lining and permanent odor.
- Used shoes are poor value if the midsole is already shaped to another foot.

### Electronics

- Test full charge/discharge.
- Inspect USB port and battery swelling.
- Confirm maps, account ownership, subscription eligibility and firmware updates.
- Check whether the device can be legally activated for the intended region.

## 19. Modular packing lists by scenario

## Day hike in hot, rainy Shanghai

- 18–25 L daypack.
- Pack liner or electronics dry bag.
- Synthetic top and trousers/shorts.
- Light rain shell or ventilated poncho on sheltered terrain.
- Sun hat and sunscreen.
- 1.5–2.5 L water.
- Snacks plus emergency food.
- Phone with offline map.
- 5,000–10,000 mAh power bank.
- Headlamp.
- Small first aid and blister kit.
- Insect repellent.
- Whistle.
- Emergency blanket.
- Trekking poles if muddy or hilly.

**Typical load:** 3–6 kg base; 1.5–3.5 kg food/water.

## High-altitude day hike from lodge or vehicle

Add or substitute:

- Three-layer shell.
- Rain pants.
- Fleece and insulated jacket.
- Warm hat and gloves.
- Sunglasses and stronger UV protection.
- Two poles.
- More emergency insulation.
- Water treatment if relying on natural sources.
- Guide/group communication device.
- Larger food reserve.

**Typical starting load:** 6–10 kg including food and water.

## Guided or porter-supported 2–3 nights

Personal daypack:

- Clothing layers.
- Shell and rain pants.
- Water and treatment backup.
- Food supplied/required for the day.
- Navigation.
- Headlamp.
- First aid and medication.
- Emergency bivy.
- Communication method.
- Sun protection.

Transported duffel:

- Sleeping bag and pad unless supplied.
- Dry sleep clothing.
- Spare socks and hygiene kit.
- Camp footwear.
- Operator-required personal gear.

**Personal base:** approximately 5–8 kg.  
**Day-carry:** often 5–9 kg including consumables.  
Confirm duffel weight limit.

## Self-supported 2–3 nights, group

Per person:

- 45–60 L pack.
- Complete personal clothing and sleep system.
- Water containers and treatment.
- Headlamp/navigation/power.
- Personal first aid and hygiene.

Shared:

- One tent per one or two people.
- Stove and fuel.
- Group pot.
- Group first aid.
- Satellite/BeiDou device.
- Repair kit.
- Optional group shelter.

**Personal base excluding shared equipment:** approximately 7–10 kg.  
**Shared allocation carried per person:** approximately 1.5–3 kg.  
**Consumables:** approximately 2.5–4.5 kg.  
**Starting load:** approximately 11–17 kg.

## Self-supported 2–3 nights, solo

- Complete personal and group-equivalent systems.
- Two independent navigation methods.
- Stronger communications plan.
- Shelter that can be pitched alone.
- Stove and full repair kit.
- Larger emergency margin.

**Base weight:** approximately 10–15 kg.  
**Starting load:** approximately 13–20 kg.

Solo travel is not recommended for an inexperienced person on remote high-altitude routes.

## Longer trip without resupply

Add:

- 55–70 L pack if genuinely needed.
- Food organized by day.
- Reliable group stove and fuel reserve.
- Larger power system.
- More robust repair kit.
- Satellite/BeiDou communications.
- Expanded first aid.
- Additional dry storage.
- One to two days of emergency food where remoteness justifies it.
- Stronger shelter and sleep margin.
- Detailed evacuation plan.

Plan approximately **0.55–0.85 kg of food per person per day** depending on calorie density and appetite; this is a planning range, not a universal requirement.

A seven-day trip can add approximately 4–6 kg of food, plus water and fuel. Starting loads commonly reach **17–25 kg in a group** or **18–28 kg solo**. If higher, reduce duplication, share gear or use porter support rather than simply accepting an unsafe load.

## Colder, wetter and windier high-altitude summer

Compared with the mild system, add approximately 1.5–4 kg for:

- −5°C to −10°C Comfort sleeping bag.
- R 5–7 pad or foam supplement.
- Warmer insulated jacket.
- Dry sleep layers.
- Heavier gloves.
- Stronger shelter, stakes and guy lines.
- More fuel.
- Greater waterproof storage.

## 21. Prioritized purchase sequence

### Phase 1 — Immediate Shanghai day-hiking core

1. Trail footwear and socks.
2. 20–30 L daypack.
3. Synthetic hiking clothing.
4. Rain shell and rain pants.
5. Headlamp.
6. Water containers.
7. Phone navigation and power bank.
8. First aid and blister kit.
9. Sun/insect protection.
10. Whistle and emergency insulation.
11. Trekking poles.

Test these through repeated hot, humid and rainy hikes.

### Phase 2 — Basic overnight system

1. Tent suitable for sheltered three-season use.
2. Pad with credible R-value.
3. ISO-rated 0°C-class bag unless route data indicates otherwise.
4. 50–60 L fitted pack.
5. Stove, cookware and water filter.
6. Dry sleep clothing and hygiene system.
7. Repair kit.

Complete at least two local overnight shakedowns.

### Phase 3 — High-altitude upgrades

Only after route details:

1. Warmer sleeping bag.
2. Higher-R pad or foam supplement.
3. Warmer insulated jacket and gloves.
4. Stronger shelter/stakes.
5. Larger pack if genuinely necessary.
6. Satellite/BeiDou device or rental.
7. Gaiters or microspikes.
8. Group emergency equipment.

### Phase 4 — Skills and logistics

Budget for:

- Wilderness first-aid training.
- Navigation practice.
- Guided introductory altitude trip.
- Medical consultation.
- Insurance.
- Acclimatization days.
- Operator quality.

These usually improve safety more than replacing a 1.8 kg tent with a 1.3 kg tent.

## 22. Pre-trip, departure and emergency checklists

## Pre-trip: one to three months before

- [ ] Obtain exact route and GPX.
- [ ] Confirm maximum and sleeping elevations.
- [ ] Confirm expected and worst-plausible nighttime temperature.
- [ ] Confirm permits, nationality restrictions and guide requirements.
- [ ] Confirm current park/camping/stove rules.
- [ ] Get operator equipment and porter-weight list in writing.
- [ ] Confirm fuel type and local supply.
- [ ] Confirm tent and sleeping-system specifications.
- [ ] Arrange insurance covering trekking altitude, rescue and evacuation.
- [ ] Discuss altitude history and medication with a clinician.
- [ ] Identify evacuation roads, clinics and emergency contacts.
- [ ] Confirm mobile and satellite/BeiDou coverage.
- [ ] Complete progressive loaded hiking training.
- [ ] Complete rain, tent, sleep, stove, filter and navigation tests.
- [ ] Break in footwear.
- [ ] Repair or replace failing equipment.
- [ ] Record serial numbers and receipts.
- [ ] Share itinerary and overdue plan with a trusted contact.

## Pre-departure: 24–72 hours before trailhead

- [ ] Check CMA/local forecasts and warnings.
- [ ] Check landslide, debris-flow, road and park alerts.
- [ ] Reconfirm route opening with park/operator.
- [ ] Reconfirm permits and identification.
- [ ] Reconfirm fuel and transport restrictions.
- [ ] Weigh pack, food, water and shared equipment separately.
- [ ] Waterproof sleeping bag, electronics and dry clothing.
- [ ] Charge all devices.
- [ ] Download maps and test airplane-mode navigation.
- [ ] Test headlamp and communicator.
- [ ] Confirm subscription/account status.
- [ ] Pack two ignition sources.
- [ ] Inspect stove seals, tent poles, stakes and pad.
- [ ] Check medication.
- [ ] Fill emergency-contact card.
- [ ] Agree turnaround, check-in and emergency signals.
- [ ] Review AMS/HACE/HAPE signs with the entire group.
- [ ] Confirm who carries each shared item.

## Daily trail check

- [ ] Weather and local warnings.
- [ ] Group symptoms and sleep quality.
- [ ] Water and fuel status.
- [ ] Route and turnaround time.
- [ ] Stream conditions.
- [ ] Battery status.
- [ ] Shelter and evacuation alternatives.
- [ ] No person ascends with worsening altitude symptoms.

## Emergency checklist

1. **Stop:** prevent the situation from worsening.
2. **Assess:** airway, breathing, circulation, bleeding, temperature and altitude symptoms.
3. **Protect:** move only if exposed to rockfall, flood, lightning or another immediate hazard.
4. **Shelter:** add insulation, rain protection and ground insulation.
5. **Communicate:** provide coordinates, altitude, symptoms, group size and weather.
6. **Navigate:** identify the safest descent and evacuation route.
7. **Altitude illness:** do not ascend; descend urgently for severe or worsening symptoms.
8. **Account for group:** do not leave anyone alone.
9. **Conserve power:** use scheduled communication windows.
10. **Document:** time, symptoms, medication, vital signs and messages.
11. **Activate SOS early** when delay materially increases risk.
12. **Do not cancel rescue casually** unless communication with responders is clear and the situation is truly resolved.

## Conclusion

The best first system is a **balanced, modular three-season trekking setup**, not an expedition-mountaineering package. For self-supported use, target a fitted 50–60 L pack, robust rain protection, light fleece plus hooded insulation, ordinary terrain-appropriate hiking footwear, two poles, a double-wall tent, an ISO-rated 0°C-class sleeping bag, an ASTM-tested R 4–5 pad, a reliable canister stove, filter plus chemical backup, redundant navigation and a comprehensive personal emergency core.

Delay colder bags, stronger tents, large packs, traction and satellite hardware until the route’s sleeping elevation, weather, permit status and operator provision are known. For colder high camps, upgrade the sleep system and insulation rather than relying on wearing every item inside an inadequate bag.

Equipment cannot create acclimatization, identify unstable slopes, make floodwater safe, authorize entry to a protected area or coordinate an evacuation by itself. Conservative ascent, early turnarounds, local guidance, practiced skills, medical advice and an honest willingness to descend remain the most important parts of the system.
