# Entrepreneurship learning strategy for the next 1–2 years

## Executive recommendation

Given your MBA-level background, professional experience, Shanghai base, variable schedule, and preference for early market experiments, **do not begin with another broad degree**. Your highest-return approach is a portfolio:

1. **A lightweight online curriculum** to establish a shared operating vocabulary.
2. **Continuous customer discovery and small venture experiments** as the core learning mechanism.
3. **A small mentor and founder network**, combining Shanghai relationships with global online access.
4. **Selective executive modules** only when a specific capability gap emerges.
5. **A pre-accelerator or accelerator only after evidence and commitment reach the appropriate level.**
6. Reconsider a part-time entrepreneurship degree after 6–9 months only if you discover that you need its credential, sustained cohort, or institutional network enough to justify the workload.

A sensible initial allocation is approximately:

- **50–60%:** customer discovery and experiments
- **20–25%:** structured courses and case work
- **15–20%:** mentors, peers, and local ecosystem
- **5–10%:** reflection, documentation, and decision reviews

This is not a rigid weekly allocation. It is a portfolio across each six- to eight-week cycle.

## 2. What representative programs show

### Degree route

HEC Paris currently presents online MSc and Executive MSc programs in Innovation and Entrepreneurship. They run for **18–24 months, contain 20 courses, and estimate about 20 hours per week**. Tuition is listed as **€22,950 for the MSc and €24,950 for the Executive MSc**. The curriculum includes team projects, industry mentoring, business development, pitch training, business games, and a thesis; HEC also offers shorter university certificates that may later receive degree credit. [citation:HEC Paris Executive MSc & MSc in Innovation and Entrepreneurship](https://www.coursera.org/degrees/omie)

This illustrates both the attraction and the problem:

- It provides a credential, structured progression, and institutional community.
- But 20 hours per week is not naturally modular and is likely to compete with family time and venture experiments.
- Because you already possess an MBA-level foundation, some broad business content may duplicate existing knowledge.
- Its ROI becomes compelling only if you strongly value the credential, cohort, or HEC ecosystem—not merely additional classroom content.

CEIBS provides a useful local comparison. Its program overview distinguishes full-time and part-time degrees from executive education; executive education is offered in Shanghai and other locations as **three-to-four-day or modular certificate programs**, while its listed part-time MBA is 24 months and based primarily in Shenzhen with Shanghai or overseas electives. [citation:CEIBS Programmes Overview](https://www.ceibs.edu/programmes)

**Verdict:** Keep degrees as an explicit later choice. Do not reject them categorically, but require a higher burden of proof than for short modules or experiments.

A degree should pass all four tests:

1. The cohort contains people you genuinely want as long-term collaborators.
2. Its applied venture work can be directed toward your own validated opportunity.
3. The credential has a specific expected use.
4. You can protect the required hours without eliminating customer work or regular family time.

### Non-degree and executive education

Short university certificates or executive modules are better suited to your circumstances when they solve a **defined** problem. Appropriate topics might include:

- Entrepreneurial finance and capitalization
- China-specific startup legal and regulatory structures
- B2B enterprise sales
- Technology commercialization
- Product management for nontechnical founders
- Founder negotiation and equity allocation
- Scaling teams from 5 to 50

They are less attractive when marketed as a general introduction to entrepreneurship. You likely do not need another broad overview.

HEC’s stackable certificate pathway demonstrates a useful risk-control model: take a shorter program first, then count it toward a degree if you later decide that the larger commitment is worthwhile. [citation:HEC Paris Executive MSc & MSc in Innovation and Entrepreneurship](https://www.coursera.org/degrees/omie)

**Value:** concentrated expertise, high-quality peers, and manageable travel.

**Limitation:** participants can leave with better frameworks but no better evidence about whether customers will buy. Require any executive module to produce a live deliverable, such as a pricing test, investor model, channel experiment, or founder agreement.

### Self-paced online learning

Y Combinator’s Startup School is a strong no-cost starting point: it is self-paced, estimates approximately **seven weeks at one to two hours per week**, and covers MVP planning, funding, user growth, team leadership, and founder issues. It also includes progress tracking and a cofounder-matching platform. [citation:YC Startup School](https://www.startupschool.org/)

Udacity’s free *How to Build a Startup* contains 14 lessons built around Steve Blank’s Customer Development method and explicitly emphasizes getting “out of the building” to gather and iterate on feedback. [citation:Udacity – How to Build a Startup](https://www.udacity.com/course/how-to-build-a-startup--ep245)

The University of Maryland’s Coursera entrepreneurship specialization provides a more conventional sequence: opportunity recognition, commercialization, venture financing, term sheets, investor pitches, and a capstone involving a customer-validated business model and investor pitch. [citation:Entrepreneurship: Launching an Innovative Business](https://www.coursera.org/specializations/entrepreneurship)

**Value:** inexpensive, modular, and easy to consume in fragmented time.

**Limitation:** completion can create a false sense of progress. Watching content is not venture validation.

Use a strict rule:

> For every hour of online instruction, complete at least one hour of observable external work—an interview, experiment, prototype test, sales conversation, or evidence review.

Do not complete multiple overlapping startup courses. Select one foundation course, then use individual modules only when the venture creates a need.

### Mentoring and networking

MicroMentor offers free connections between entrepreneurs and volunteer mentors. It reports an average of 20 mentoring hours per entrepreneur, while explicitly noting that it provides advice rather than funding. [citation:MicroMentor](https://www.micromentor.org/)

Startup Grind has operated in Shanghai since 2013 and organizes events connecting founders, corporate innovators, investors, and other ecosystem participants. Its Shanghai chapter is currently operated by PROPELLER, with an emphasis on China’s AI and robotics ecosystem and cross-border venture building. [citation:Startup Grind Shanghai](https://www.startupgrind.com/shanghai/)

YC Startup School also offers a global cofounder-matching service, claiming more than 100,000 matches, although a match should be treated only as an introduction—not evidence of cofounder compatibility. [citation:YC Startup School](https://www.startupschool.org/)

For you, the ideal network is not the largest one. Build a **five-person learning board**:

- One founder who has built and sold in mainland China
- One product/customer-discovery practitioner
- One operator with domain expertise in your emerging problem area
- One technical/product-building adviser
- One finance, legal, or fundraising adviser, added later

Avoid asking, “Is this a good idea?” Send mentors a one-page evidence brief:

- Hypothesis
- Experiments completed
- Results
- What surprised you
- Decision currently under consideration
- One or two specific questions

That changes mentoring from general conversation into decision support.

### Practical venture-building

A useful operating framework is Strategyzer’s *Testing Business Ideas*: identify assumptions, prioritize the riskiest ones, and choose experiments based on cost, time, and strength of evidence. Its library contains 43 experiment types and integrates assumptions mapping with the Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas. [citation:Strategyzer – Testing Business Ideas](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/testing-business-ideas-book)

The NSF I-Corps model provides evidence for the value of intensive customer discovery. It uses a seven-week experiential program to move technical teams beyond the laboratory and reduce commercialization risk. NSF reports that more than 2,500 teams have participated and that nearly 1,400 formed startups, although those aggregate figures do not prove that the program caused every outcome. [citation:NSF I-Corps](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/i-corps)

**Value:** strongest fit for your goals because it produces real customer and market evidence.

**Limitation:** self-directed work can drift. Counter this with fixed experiment cycles, external review, and written continuation criteria.

### Pre-accelerators and accelerators

Founder Institute is positioned before seed accelerators and works with founders moving from idea to traction. However, its own FAQ says that participation requires **at least 20 hours per week on average**, including a mandatory weekly session, networking, sprint work, and team meetings. The referenced 2026 virtual cohort listed an early fee of **US$1,199**, rising to US$1,649, while the FAQ also describes a 2.5% company pledge. Only about 30% of participants reportedly complete the program. [citation:Founder Institute FAQ](https://fi.co/faq) [citation:Founder Institute Program](https://fi.co/join)

That makes it potentially valuable but not currently compatible with an unspecified, variable schedule. Consider it only after:

- You have selected a problem,
- You have protected 20 hours per week in a trial month,
- Your family agrees with the temporary load,
- You understand all fee, equity, intellectual-property, and entity terms.

Techstars describes its accelerators as **three months of intensive, mentorship-driven support** connecting early-stage companies to expertise, community, capital, and investors. [citation:Techstars Accelerators](https://www.techstars.com/accelerators)

YC is even less compatible with maintaining an ordinary job. Its Winter 2027 batch is planned in person in San Francisco with regular local meetups, dedicated partner interaction, founder groups, and investor introductions. [citation:Apply to Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/apply) YC’s current standard deal invests US$500,000: US$125,000 for 7% plus US$375,000 on an uncapped most-favored-nation SAFE. It may also require a company from another jurisdiction to establish a parent in the United States, Canada, Cayman Islands, or Singapore. [citation:Y Combinator Standard Deal](https://www.ycombinator.com/deal)

Therefore:

- **Startup School:** suitable now.
- **Founder Institute or similar pre-accelerator:** possible after validation and a workload test.
- **Techstars, YC, or comparable seed accelerator:** only when you are prepared to prioritize the company and accept investment terms.
- Do not join an accelerator merely to “learn entrepreneurship.” Equity is too expensive a tuition payment.

EO Accelerator is even later-stage: applicants must already own an operating business with annual gross revenue between **US$250,000 and US$1 million**. Its listed fee is US$1,750 plus possible local fees, with quarterly learning events and peer networking. [citation:EO Accelerator](https://eonetwork.org/eo-accelerator/) It is irrelevant now but could become useful after genuine revenue traction.

# 4. Modular 18-month plan

This is designed around cycles rather than fixed academic semesters. Each cycle has a minimum viable version, a standard version, and an optional stretch layer.

## Phase 0 — Establish operating boundaries

**Timing:** Weeks 1–2  
**Purpose:** Protect work and family obligations before adding startup activity.

Create a written “entrepreneurship operating agreement” with yourself and your family:

- Two protected family blocks each week that startup work cannot displace
- One regular 90-minute venture block
- Two or three 25–40-minute fragmented-time blocks
- One optional weekend fieldwork block, scheduled in advance
- A monthly maximum for experiment spending
- No use of employer confidential information, data, code, customer lists, or working time
- Review employment terms covering inventions, conflicts, outside work, and intellectual property; obtain local legal advice if necessary

### Capacity levels

Because your sustainable hours vary, use three operating modes:

**Maintenance week: 2–3 hours**

- One interview or evidence review
- One short lesson or reading
- Update experiment log
- No new major commitment

**Standard week: 5–7 hours**

- Two interviews or one experiment
- One structured learning block
- One mentor/network touchpoint every two weeks
- Weekly synthesis

**Sprint week: 8–12 hours**

- Prototype, landing page, observational study, or multiple interviews
- Use sparingly and schedule it with family in advance

Never compensate for a missed week by sacrificing the next family weekend. Resume with the next modular task.

## Phase 1 — Build the method and map problem spaces

**Timing:** Months 1–3  
**Primary objective:** Identify two or three promising problem areas, not startup ideas.

### Structured learning

Complete only one foundational sequence:

- YC Startup School, approximately one to two hours per week for seven weeks [citation:YC Startup School](https://www.startupschool.org/), or
- Selected customer-development lessons from Udacity’s free startup course [citation:Udacity – How to Build a Startup](https://www.udacity.com/course/how-to-build-a-startup--ep245)

Use fragmented time for video and reading. Reserve uninterrupted blocks for interviews and synthesis.

### Practical work

Select three exploratory arenas using:

- Your legitimate professional exposure
- Problems experienced by people you can reach
- Structural or behavioral changes you observe
- Your founder-market advantage
- Ability to test without leaving your job

Do not rank sectors by excitement alone. For each arena, assess:

1. Frequency and severity of the problem
2. Existing workaround and current spending
3. Accessibility of potential users
4. Ability to run a small test
5. Your insight or unfair access advantage
6. Regulatory and conflict-of-interest risk
7. Plausible business buyer and budget owner

Conduct **18–24 discovery conversations** across the three areas. Ask about recent behavior rather than reactions to a proposed solution:

- “Tell me about the last time this happened.”
- “What did you do?”
- “What did it cost in time, money, risk, or frustration?”
- “Who else was involved?”
- “What have you already tried?”
- “Who controls the budget?”

### Network

Attend no more than one targeted Shanghai event per month. Startup Grind Shanghai is one available entry point, but judge events by the density of relevant operators and founders, not attendance size. [citation:Startup Grind Shanghai](https://www.startupgrind.com/shanghai/)

Recruit one experienced founder or customer-discovery mentor. A volunteer platform such as MicroMentor can supplement local introductions. [citation:MicroMentor](https://www.micromentor.org/)

### Month 3 gate

Continue with an arena only if you have evidence of:

- A repeated, consequential problem
- A clearly identifiable user or buyer
- Existing expenditure, workaround, or material cost
- Access to at least 15 additional relevant interviewees
- A cheap next experiment

If none passes, repeat Phase 1 with new arenas. That is progress, not failure.

---

## Phase 2 — Validate problems and business-model assumptions

**Timing:** Months 4–6  
**Primary objective:** Reduce three major uncertainties: desirability, buyer behavior, and access.

Choose the best one or two problem areas. Build an assumptions map covering:

- User
- Problem
- Current alternative
- Buyer
- Decision process
- Value proposition
- Channel
- Pricing
- Delivery feasibility
- Legal or regulatory barrier

Use Strategyzer’s principle of choosing tests by cost, time, and evidential strength rather than building the most impressive prototype. [citation:Strategyzer – Testing Business Ideas](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/testing-business-ideas-book)

### Experiments

Run at least three different types:

1. **Problem evidence:** interviews, observation, workflow reconstruction
2. **Demand evidence:** landing page, invitation, waitlist, demo request, or letter of intent
3. **Payment evidence:** paid diagnostic, refundable deposit, preorder, pilot agreement, or budget conversation

A “yes, interesting” comment is not validation. Rank evidence approximately as:

1. Money or signed commitment
2. Significant customer time or data access
3. Introduction to a decision-maker
4. Repeated use
5. Waitlist or contact details
6. Stated enthusiasm

### Learning

Take only gap-specific modules. Examples:

- Interview design if conversations are producing vague results
- No-code or prototyping if users need to interact with something
- B2B sales if buyer access is the obstacle
- Unit economics if pricing appears feasible
- Entrepreneurial finance only if external capital may be necessary

### Month 6 gate

Select one path:

- **Advance:** repeated problem plus credible buyer behavior
- **Refine:** real problem but weak segment, channel, or value proposition
- **Stop:** polite interest without costly behavior or commitment
- **Explore another arena:** evidence remains weak after disciplined testing

Do not enroll in a large program to avoid making this decision.

## Phase 3 — Test a narrow solution and willingness to pay

**Timing:** Months 7–9  
**Primary objective:** Deliver value manually before building extensively.

Create the smallest testable offer:

- Concierge service
- Wizard-of-Oz workflow
- Spreadsheet or messaging-based prototype
- No-code demonstration
- Manual report or analysis
- Paid workshop
- Narrow B2B pilot

Targets should be tailored to the market, but a useful default is:

- 5–10 serious prototype users
- 3–5 buyer or budget-owner conversations
- At least one paid or contract-like commitment
- Documented before/after outcome
- Clear reasons for non-adoption

Track:

- Acquisition source
- Conversion at each step
- Time to first value
- Usage or completion
- Customer effort required
- Price response
- Delivery cost
- Retention or repeated need
- Objections and loss reasons

### Team learning

Do not select a cofounder through networking chemistry alone. If someone looks promising, conduct a **four-to-six-week working trial** with:

- One concrete deliverable
- Explicit time commitments
- Clear decision rights
- A conflict or retrospective discussion
- Disclosure of personal constraints
- No permanent equity allocation before testing compatibility and receiving legal advice

### Month 9 decision review

This is the first point at which a substantial paid program becomes reasonable.

Assess:

- Is there a venture worth accelerating?
- Is your constraint skill, accountability, network, technical execution, or time?
- Would a program produce evidence faster than direct work?
- Can you sustain its real workload?
- Are investment or equity terms justified by the network and capital?

---

## Phase 4 — Choose between three pathways

**Timing:** Months 10–12

### Path A: Evidence is still exploratory

Remain in independent experimentation.

- Run a second discovery cycle
- Add a live online cohort for accountability if needed
- Do not apply to equity accelerators
- Consider a short university certificate only if it directly strengthens the next experiments

### Path B: Promising opportunity, but limited weekly capacity

Continue as a disciplined side venture:

- One target segment
- One offer
- One acquisition channel
- One primary metric
- Monthly mentor review
- Quarterly family and workload review

A low-intensity live cohort or executive module may fit, but reject anything requiring 15–20 weekly hours.

### Path C: Strong evidence and willingness to increase commitment

Evaluate a pre-accelerator.

Founder Institute is an example of a virtual pre-seed program, but its minimum 20-hour weekly commitment means you should first conduct a **four-week rehearsal** at that workload. [citation:Founder Institute FAQ](https://fi.co/faq)

Before joining, examine:

- Mandatory attendance
- Shanghai timezone fit
- Total weekly work
- Refund policy
- Equity or warrant terms
- IP provisions
- Entity requirements
- Mentor relevance
- Alumni you can independently contact
- Number of participants who reach your desired outcome
- Whether you retain control over fundraising timing

## Phase 5 — Build repeatability

**Timing:** Months 13–18  
**Primary objective:** Determine whether this is becoming a company or remains an experiment.

Focus learning on the bottleneck.

| Bottleneck | Learning intervention | Required field evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Users care but do not buy | Pricing, enterprise sales, procurement | Paid pilot or purchase process |
| Users buy but do not continue | Product discovery, onboarding, retention | Repeated use and renewal intent |
| Demand exists but delivery is manual | Product architecture and operations | Reduced delivery time without quality loss |
| Customer acquisition is expensive | Positioning, channels, growth experiments | Repeatable lead source |
| Founder capacity is inadequate | Cofounder or early-team design | Successful work trial |
| Capital is genuinely required | Fundraising, term sheets, financial model | Credible milestones and use of funds |
| China/global expansion is central | Local legal, data, tax, and cross-border expertise | Feasible entity and compliance path |

### Fundraising sequence

Learn funding mechanisms, but do not make pitching the primary activity before customer evidence.

By Month 15–18, prepare:

- 24-month financial model
- Milestone-based use of funds
- Unit economics or credible path to them
- Customer evidence repository
- Market and competitor analysis
- Cap-table scenarios
- Founder roles and vesting proposal
- Data room outline
- “Why capital now?” memo

If the venture has genuine traction and you are prepared to prioritize it, then examine seed accelerators such as Techstars or YC. Techstars describes a three-month intensive format; YC’s current program requires in-person participation in San Francisco, making either a major commitment decision rather than an educational add-on. [citation:Techstars Accelerators](https://www.techstars.com/accelerators) [citation:Apply to Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/apply)

## Optional Phase 6 — Months 19–24

Choose one of four outcomes.

### 1. Commit to the venture

Use this period for incorporation, initial hiring, repeatable sales, accelerator applications, or fundraising. Obtain China-qualified legal, tax, employment, data, and IP advice before implementation.

### 2. Continue as a side venture

Set narrow milestones for another six months. Avoid indefinite “promising” status; specify the customer or revenue evidence that would justify greater commitment.

### 3. Stop and recycle the learning

Write a venture post-mortem:

- Which assumptions failed?
- Which customer group remained accessible?
- What assets, relationships, or insights can transfer?
- Which problem deserves a second cycle?

Then begin another discovery cycle without repeating the general coursework.

### 4. Choose formal education deliberately

A degree or executive certificate may now make sense if the experiments reveal that you need:

- A durable international founder network
- A credential for a planned career transition
- Deep exposure to a technology or commercialization ecosystem
- Sustained external accountability
- A structured bridge into a new geography

At this stage, you can evaluate programs using actual needs rather than hypothetical preferences.

# 5. Program-selection scorecard

Before paying for any program, score it from 1–5 and multiply by the suggested weight.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Direct relevance to current venture bottleneck | 20 |
| Requires work with real customers or venture data | 20 |
| Schedule resilience and Shanghai timezone fit | 15 |
| Mentor relevance and accessibility | 10 |
| Peer quality and likelihood of durable relationships | 10 |
| Ability to test before making a large commitment | 5 |
| Total cost, including travel and displaced venture time | 10 |
| Evidence of participant outcomes | 5 |
| Credential value for a specific intended use | 5 |

Apply three vetoes regardless of total score:

- It consumes so much time that customer contact stops.
- It requires equity before it offers venture-specific value.
- It relies primarily on prestige or generic content to justify the price.

# 6. Practical learning system

Maintain a simple venture evidence repository with five documents:

1. **Problem portfolio:** all areas considered and why they advanced or stopped.
2. **Interview log:** date, participant type, behavior, quotes, current workaround, and referrals.
3. **Assumption register:** assumption, importance, confidence, experiment, result, next decision.
4. **Experiment ledger:** cost, time, prediction, outcome, evidence strength, and learning.
5. **Decision journal:** continue, pivot, pause, or stop—and why.

Use a six-week cadence:

- **Week 1:** identify the highest-risk assumption
- **Weeks 2–4:** conduct interviews or experiments
- **Week 5:** analyze results and obtain mentor critique
- **Week 6:** decide, document, and schedule the next cycle

This makes the plan resilient to busy periods. A delayed experiment can resume without losing the entire course sequence.

---

## Bottom line

Your best first-year route is:

**One lightweight online foundation + systematic customer discovery + inexpensive experiments + a small Shanghai/global mentor network.**

Do not pay degree-level tuition until you can identify a benefit that experiments, mentoring, and focused executive modules cannot provide. Do not join an accelerator until you have a venture worth accelerating and enough capacity to treat the program as company-building. By Month 9, accumulated customer evidence should determine whether the second year emphasizes another exploration cycle, a live cohort, a targeted executive module, a pre-accelerator, or an actual company launch.
